## Definition

How news is made refers to the institutional processes, professional norms, technological systems, and economic incentives through which real-world events are observed, selected, reported, edited, and distributed to the public as "news." Journalism is not a passive mirror reflecting reality neutrally; it is an active process of cultural and political production. Reporters and news organizations operate through organizational filters, professional codes of ethics, and deadlines that determine which events are deemed "newsworthy" and how stories are framed (Galtung and Ruge, 1965; Schudson, 2011).

## Why it matters

In contemporary democratic societies, ordinary citizens cannot directly witness government cabinet debates, foreign wars, corporate board decisions, or courtroom trials; they rely almost exclusively on news media for the political information needed to evaluate their leaders and exercise [citizenship](../rights/citizenship). Understanding the production mechanics of news empowers citizens with media literacy: the ability to recognize what has been included, what has been omitted, whose perspectives are prioritized, and how commercial pressures or state interference shape the daily flow of information.

## How it works

### News values: What makes an event news?

In their classic sociological study, Johan Galtung and Mari Holmboe Ruge (1965) identified key "news values" — criteria that editors and journalists consciously and unconsciously use to select stories:
- **Impact and scale**: how many people are affected by the event.
- **Proximity**: geographical and cultural closeness to the intended audience.
- **Conflict and drama**: political battles, scandals, and dramatic confrontations receive far greater prominence than gradual, constructive developments.
- **Prominence**: events involving heads of state, celebrities, or powerful institutions are prioritized over ordinary people's experiences.
- **Timeliness and novelty**: rare, sudden, or shocking events are favored over long-term structural trends (e.g., plane crashes vs. preventable chronic disease).

### The editorial pipeline

A typical news story moves through four institutional stages (Schudson, 2011):
1. **Sourcing**: reporters cultivate sources (government spokespersons, police, whistleblowers, witnesses, press releases). Reliance on official sources often gives state authorities structural power to set the news agenda.
2. **Fact-checking and verification**: cross-referencing claims against independent documents, multiple witnesses, and official records.
3. **Editing and gatekeeping**: news editors determine headline framing, word length, placement (front page vs. buried section), and visual imagery.
4. **Distribution and platform algorithms**: digital algorithms prioritize content that maximizes user clicks, emotional arousal, and time-on-page.

## Contested points

- **Objectivity vs. Advocacy**: the traditional 20th-century ideal of journalistic "objectivity" demands that reporters remain detached, presenting "both sides" neutrally. Critics argue that mechanical balance often creates "false equivalence" (e.g., balancing overwhelming scientific consensus on climate change with denialist lobby claims). Many journalists today advocate "fairness and truth-seeking" over performative neutrality.
- **Commercial incentives and the attention economy**: as the collapse of print advertising forced media outlets into digital subscription and click-driven models, newsrooms faced intense commercial pressure to produce sensationalist clickbait, outrage-inducing headlines, and celebrity gossip at the expense of substantive investigative reporting.
- **State ownership vs. Corporate oligopoly**: state-owned broadcasters (e.g., BBC, PBS) face accusations of pro-government bias or dependency on state funding. Conversely, corporate media conglomerates (e.g., News Corp) face criticism that wealthy media proprietors manipulate editorial policy to protect their private business empires and tax privileges (Herman and Chomsky, 1988).

## Examples

- **The Pentagon Papers (1971)**: *The New York Times* and *The Washington Post* published classified defense documents leaked by Daniel Ellsberg detailing secret government deception regarding the Vietnam War, demonstrating how investigative journalism serves public accountability despite intense White House pressure.
- **The 24-Hour Cable News Revolution (1980s–1990s)**: the launch of CNN and subsequent partisan networks (Fox News, MSNBC) transformed news from once-daily evening broadcasts into a continuous cycle demanding constant breaking-news alerts, live punditry panels, and emotional engagement.
- **Iran**: broadcast media is constitutionally monopolized by the state through Islamic Republic of Iran Broadcasting (IRIB / صدا و سیما), whose head is appointed directly by the Supreme Leader. IRIB operates strictly as an arm of state communication and ideological guidance, prompting millions of Iranians to turn to foreign-based satellite channels (BBC Persian, Iran International) or digital social channels for alternative political reporting, which the state regularly attempts to jam or censor.
