title: Propaganda and disinformation
description: The methods, psychology, and institutional architecture behind coordinated political manipulation and false information.

## Definition
Propaganda is the deliberate, systematic dissemination of information, allegations, or emotional appeals designed to shape public perceptions, manipulate cognitions, and direct behaviour toward a specific political objective. Disinformation is a subset of information disorder referring specifically to false or misleading content manufactured and spread deliberately to deceive, confuse, or cause harm.

Scholars distinguish disinformation from misinformation (inadvertent sharing of falsehoods) and malinformation (weaponising genuine private facts). Propaganda employs selected distortions to construct an all-encompassing narrative legitimising power or discrediting rivals.

## Why it matters
The systematic spread of propaganda and disinformation undermines the cognitive foundations of democratic society. When public debate ceases to be grounded in shared factual reality, democratic deliberation collapses into tribal hostility, and public cynicism paralyzes civic resistance.

In authoritarian systems, state propaganda operates as a core instrument of survival, suppressing dissent, justifying political violence, and constructing artificial popular legitimacy. In international politics, cross-border disinformation campaigns are used as asymmetric weapons of hybrid warfare, aimed at polarizing foreign societies, swaying democratic elections, and eroding trust in multilateral alliances.

## How it works
Modern propaganda and disinformation campaigns exploit psychological vulnerabilities and digital network architectures through several coordinated mechanisms:

1. **Emotional Priming**: Messages are engineered to evoke intense fear, anger, or existential threat. Evolutionary psychology shows that threat-based narratives bypass deliberate critical thinking, accelerating uncritical belief and viral sharing.
2. **Computational Propaganda**: Coordinated networks of automated social media bots, paid troll farms, and deceptive sockpuppet accounts artificially inflate the reach of manufactured talking points, creating an illusion of widespread popular consensus (astroturfing).
3. **The Firehose of Falsehood**: As identified by researchers studying contemporary state propaganda, actors flood the information space with high volumes of conflicting, rapid, and repetitive falsehoods. The goal is not necessarily to convince citizens of a single lie, but to overwhelm them until they conclude that objective truth is unknowable.
4. **Information Laundering**: Fabricated stories originate on fringe message boards or state-controlled proxy outlets, gradually infiltrate hyperpartisan blogs, and are eventually quoted by mainstream politicians or commentators.
5. **Targeted Microtargeting**: Leveraging digital data brokers to identify psychologically susceptible demographic slices and delivering customized manipulative messaging tailored to their specific anxieties.

## Contested points
The regulation of propaganda and disinformation presents profound legal and ethical dilemmas in free societies.

One major controversy centres on state regulation versus freedom of expression. Civil libertarians argue that government agencies or state-mandated regulatory bodies cannot be trusted to define "disinformation" impartially. They warn that granting authorities the power to censor or penalise "fake news" inevitably hands ruling parties a legal weapon to suppress legitimate political dissent, investigative journalism, and inconvenient criticism.

Conversely, national security theorists and democratic defenders contend that leaving weaponized disinformation unregulated allows hostile foreign intelligence services and malicious domestic actors to destabilize constitutional orders with impunity. They argue that algorithmic platforms should be legally compelled to maintain transparent recommendation algorithms, curb inauthentic coordinated behaviour, and label state-backed media.

A second debate surrounds counter-propaganda. Some strategic communications experts advocate state-sponsored strategic messaging to actively rebut foreign disinformation. Critics counter that state counter-propaganda risks polluting democratic discourse further, arguing that independent investigative journalism, media literacy education, and strong public broadcasters are far more effective and legitimate safeguards.

## Examples
A foundational 20th-century manifestation was the Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda in Nazi Germany, headed by Joseph Goebbels. Utilizing radio, cinema, and newspapers, the regime systematically dehumanised Jewish populations and political opponents, manufacturing public consent for aggressive expansionism and the Holocaust.

In modern statecraft, the Russian Internet Research Agency (IRA) in Saint Petersburg orchestrated large-scale computational disinformation campaigns during the 2016 US presidential election and the 2014 annexation of Crimea. By infiltrating both progressive and conservative social media groups with incendiary racial, cultural, and political content, the operation sought to exacerbate domestic divisions.

In Iran, state propaganda is orchestrated through the Supreme National Security Council, the Ministry of Intelligence, the IRGC Cyber Command, and state broadcaster IRIB. The Iranian state maintains thousands of coordinated accounts and proxy media sites (such as the International Union of Virtual Media, uncovered by independent researchers in 2018) to spread state narratives globally. Domestically, the state enforces the concept of "Jihad of Clarification" (جهاد تبیین), officially framing state ideology as sacred truth while criminalising independent reporting on protests, economic mismanagement, and human rights violations as "spreading lies" (نشر اکاذیب).
