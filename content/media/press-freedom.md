title: Press freedom and censorship
description: The institutional protections, legal frameworks, and coercive practices shaping independent journalism and state control.

## Definition
Press freedom is the fundamental principle that communication, journalism, and news media should be exercised freely without state censorship, prior restraint, political interference, or punitive retaliation. Censorship is the suppression, prohibition, or editing of speech, public communication, or information deemed objectionable, harmful, sensitive, or politically dangerous by governments, religious authorities, or other institutions holding coercive power.

Recognised internationally under Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, freedom of the press protects both the right of journalists to investigate and report information and the corresponding right of the public to receive diverse, uncensored news.

## Why it matters
Independent journalism functions as an indispensable oversight mechanism—frequently referred to as the "Fourth Estate"—holding public officials, corporate executives, and political institutions accountable. A free press exposes official corruption, investigative abuses of power, and structural failures that would otherwise remain hidden behind state secrecy.

Without press freedom, democratic accountability becomes impossible; citizens cannot cast informed votes or participate meaningfully in self-governance if public information is monopolised and curated by the ruling regime. Conversely, pervasive censorship stifles cultural innovation, conceals public health catastrophes, and enables authoritarian rule to entrench itself without challenge.

## How it works
Press freedom relies on an interlocking framework of legal guarantees, independent courts, economic pluralism, and institutional norms:

1. **Constitutional and Statutory Protections**: Legal systems shield journalists from prior restraint, establish rights to protect confidential sources (shield laws), and ensure access to state documents through Freedom of Information Acts (FOIA).
2. **Mechanisms of Censorship**: Direct censorship includes government pre-publication licensing, shutting down news outlets, jamming radio and television broadcasts, blocking websites, and expelling or jailing reporters.
3. **Indirect and Structural Coercion**: Autocrats frequently deploy "soft censorship," directing state advertising contracts exclusively to loyalist outlets, using arbitrary tax audits to bankrupt independent publications, or facilitating the purchase of independent news organisations by politically connected oligarchs.
4. **Physical and Legal Intimidation**: Coercion operates through strategic lawsuits against public participation (SLAPPs), weaponised criminal defamation statutes, physical surveillance, and extrajudicial violence.
5. **Self-Censorship**: When journalists observe peers being arrested or assassinated without consequence, fear induces self-censorship, silencing critical reporting without requiring formal state decrees.

## Contested points
The boundaries and permissible limits of press freedom remain an area of continuous legal and political contestation.

One central debate involves national security and state secrets. Governments argue that state survival requires protecting classified military intelligence and diplomatic communications, asserting that publishing leaked classified documents endangers lives and jeopardises foreign relations. Press freedom defenders counter that national security is routinely weaponised as a pretext to conceal official wrongdoing, war crimes, and governmental incompetence, arguing that public interest must always take precedence over institutional embarrassment.

Another dispute involves commercial media ownership and pluralism. Critics argue that private capital ownership creates its own subtle censorship, where the corporate interests of wealthy media moguls and advertising sponsors dictate newsroom priorities and marginalize working-class perspectives. Proponents of free-market media counter that private competition remains the only effective safeguard against complete state capture.

## Examples
A landmark legal victory for press freedom occurred in the 1971 US Supreme Court case *New York Times Co. v. United States* (the Pentagon Papers case). The Nixon administration attempted to enjoin *The New York Times* and *The Washington Post* from publishing a classified Department of Defense history of US military involvement in Vietnam. The Supreme Court ruled against prior restraint, establishing that the press exists to serve the governed, not the governors.

Internationally, the assassination of Saudi dissident journalist Jamal Khashoggi inside the Saudi Arabian consulate in Istanbul in October 2018 exemplified the lethal risks journalists face when exposing authoritarian corruption and state violence.

In Iran, the press has faced systematic repression since the 1979 Revolution. Under the Press Law of 1986 and subsequent revisions, publications are banned from criticising the Supreme Leader, insulting religious sanctities, or undermining state security. Following the mass shutdown of reformist newspapers during the "Toghif" era of 2000, independent print journalism was decimated. In 2022, journalists Niloofar Hamedi and Elaheh Mohammadi were arrested and imprisoned for months simply for reporting on the hospitalisation and funeral of Jina Mahsa Amini. Iran consistently ranks among the most repressive countries on Reporters Without Borders' World Press Freedom Index.
