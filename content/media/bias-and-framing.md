title: Bias and framing
description: An examination of how perspective, word choice, and structural narratives shape the presentation and reception of political issues.

## Definition
Bias and framing refer to the cognitive, cultural, and institutional lenses through which political events and ideas are selected, structured, and presented. Bias denotes a systematic inclination or prejudice for or against a particular group, ideology, or interpretation, which distorts impartiality. Framing refers to the process whereby communicators—such as journalists, politicians, and advocates—inevitably emphasize certain aspects of an issue while downplaying or omitting others, defining the problem and steering proposed solutions.

As Robert Entman established in communication theory, to frame is to "select some aspects of a perceived reality and make them more salient in a communicating text." Framing is not necessarily deliberate deception; because no report can contain every factual nuance, selection and prioritization are inescapable features of human language.

## Why it matters
The way political issues are framed directly shapes how citizens understand their world and evaluate leaders. When an economic reform is framed as "fostering investment and growth," public reaction is far more favourable than when the exact same policy is framed as "slashing pensions and worker protections." Framing sets the parameters of debate, defining what is considered pragmatic, radical, or common sense.

Unchecked media bias can polarize democratic electorates into antagonistic tribes consuming fundamentally divergent accounts of reality. Furthermore, institutional bias within legal systems, administrative bureaucracies, and state media can systematically entrench discriminatory power structures while creating the appearance of technical objectivity.

## How it works
Framing operates through subtle rhetorical and structural mechanisms:

1. **Selection and Salience**: Determining which events become prominent headlines and which are ignored, thereby shaping public perception of societal priorities.
2. **Lexical Choices**: The words chosen to describe actors carry profound moral weight. Labelling armed combatants as "freedom fighters," "rebels," or "terrorists" triggers differing legal and emotional reactions.
3. **Attribution of Agency**: Episodic framing presents political issues as isolated events or personal moral failings, whereas thematic framing places issues within broader structural systems and historical trends.
4. **Visual Framing**: Imagery, camera angles, and placement convey subconscious judgements; for example, camera angles can project authority or vulnerability.
5. **Audience Confirmation Bias**: Framing interacts powerfully with cognitive heuristics. Citizens gravitate toward frames that validate pre-existing worldviews while rejecting counter-evidence.

## Contested points
The nature and extent of media bias remain vigorously contested in contemporary political life.

Conservative critics argue that mainstream legacy newsrooms exhibit a pervasive cultural and liberal bias. They contend that journalist cohorts—overwhelmingly university-educated, secular, and metropolitan—subconsciously normalize progressive social values while marginalizing traditional, religious, or nationalist viewpoints.

Conversely, left-leaning critics and political economists, drawing upon Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky's "propaganda model," argue that corporate media suffer from a systemic structural bias toward corporate capital and state power. In this view, commercial advertising and concentrated ownership filter out radical challenges to the economic status quo.

A third perspective argues that the most damaging bias is newsroom conventions: the drive toward sensationalism, novel conflict, personality clashes, and "both-sidesism" (giving equal weight to opposing claims even when empirical evidence heavily favours one side).

## Examples
During the debate over the United States federal estate tax in the late 1990s and early 2000s, Republican political strategist Frank Luntz advised opponents to reframe the measure as the "death tax." By shifting the conceptual frame from an egalitarian tax on vast inherited fortunes to a punitive tax on death itself, public opposition surged across all income brackets.

In international reporting, coverage of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict frequently exemplifies divergent framing. Israeli and Western media frames historically prioritized security against terrorism and rocket fire, whereas Arab and international human rights organizations framed the situation around military occupation, apartheid, and civilian dispossession under international law.

In Iran, state media framing of popular protests illustrates systemic narrative construction. During the widespread 2022 protests following the death of Jina Mahsa Amini, official outlets consistently framed demonstrations not as spontaneous civil dissent regarding human rights and bodily autonomy, but as foreign-engineered "riots" (اغتشاشات). Conversely, independent diaspora media framed the movement through the lens of feminist revolution and popular liberation ("Woman, Life, Freedom").
