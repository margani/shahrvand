title: Evaluating sources
description: A practical guide to assessing the credibility, expertise, evidence, and motivation behind political information.
---

## Definition
Evaluating sources is the systematic practice of examining who produced information, what evidence supports it, how it was gathered, and what motivations or institutional interests shape it. In political analysis, where assertions are frequently weaponised to sway public opinion or justify policy choices, source evaluation serves as an intellectual filter distinguishing verifiable evidence from speculation, propaganda, and partisan advocacy.

Rather than accepting information at face value or dismissing it based on ideological discomfort, source evaluation relies on criteria established across journalism, historiography, and library science: authority, methodology, corroboration, timeliness, and transparent accountability.

## Why it matters
Democratic governance and informed civic participation depend on the public's ability to distinguish credible reporting from misleading assertions. Without effective evaluation, public discourse becomes vulnerable to disinformation, echo chambers, and conspiracy theories, eroding trust in democratic institutions and scientific consensus.

For individuals, uncritical consumption leads to misguided civic actions, from voting based on falsehoods to spreading inflammatory claims. Institutional decision-makers—including judges, legislators, and civil servants—rely on rigorous source assessment to craft legislation and allocate public resources without succumbing to special-interest manipulation.

## How it works
Media literacy educators commonly use structured frameworks to assess sources. One prominent methodology is lateral reading, developed by the Stanford History Education Group, which emphasizes leaving an unfamiliar website to check what independent sources say about it before reading its contents. Key evaluation criteria include:

1. **Authority and Expertise**: Identifying author and publisher credentials, documented domain expertise, and institutional transparency.
2. **Evidence and Sourcing**: Checking whether assertions are supported by primary documentation, named eyewitnesses, or empirical data rather than uncorroborated anonymous leaks.
3. **Methodology and Transparency**: Assessing whether sample sizes, research methods, and potential conflicts of interest are openly disclosed.
4. **Corroboration**: Cross-referencing claims across multiple independent outlets with divergent editorial viewpoints.
5. **Intent and Context**: Discerning whether the underlying purpose is to inform, persuade, sell, or mobilize partisan outrage.

## Contested points
The evaluation of sources involves contested interpretations regarding epistemic authority and institutional bias.

Critics of mainstream gatekeeping argue that relying heavily on established institutional authorities (such as legacy corporate media, government press releases, or elite university presses) reinforces establishment orthodoxies and marginalizes dissenting, grassroots, or dissident viewpoints. From this perspective, institutional credentials often reflect socio-economic privilege and conformity rather than unvarnished truth.

Conversely, defenders of institutional standards contend that unverified information ecosystems create epistemic chaos, where algorithmic virality rewards sensationalism over fact-checking. They argue that established institutional procedures—such as editorial liability, formal corrections, professional ethics codes, and legal accountability—remain the only reliable bulwark against systematic disinformation.

Another debate concerns fact-checking organizations. Proponents view independent fact-checkers as vital arbiters of accuracy. Critics argue that fact-checkers can exhibit subtle bias in their selection of claims to verify, their framing of truth ratings, and their conflation of disputed policy interpretations with factual errors.

## Examples
In the lead-up to the 2003 United States-led invasion of Iraq, major Western news outlets uncritically reported official intelligence leaks and unvetted defectors (such as "Curveball") alleging Iraqi weapons of mass destruction. Subsequent inquiries revealed intelligence manipulation and a failure of independent corroboration, leading to public reckonings at publications like *The New York Times* in 2004.

In the 2016 US presidential election, clickbait operators in Veles, North Macedonia, created hundreds of fabricated political websites mimicking legitimate news outlets to generate digital advertising revenue, demonstrating that professional-looking web design does not equate to credible sourcing.

In Iran, state broadcaster IRIB presents official narratives that human rights monitors regularly contest. Televised confessions of political detainees are broadcast as primary source evidence of espionage. International human rights bodies, such as Amnesty International and the UN Special Rapporteur on Iran, evaluate these broadcasts against independent testimony and forensic evidence, concluding that coerced confessions lack evidentiary value and constitute state propaganda.
