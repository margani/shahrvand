title: Reading a political speech
description: A structural and rhetorical guide to deconstructing public addresses, policy announcements, and political rhetoric.

## Definition
Reading a political speech is the critical practice of analysing the rhetorical structures, arguments, appeals, and unstated assumptions through which political figures address the public. Rather than treating an address as a spontaneous monologue, critical analysis views a political speech as a crafted strategic instrument designed to persuade, justify policy, consolidate political coalitions, or marginalise opponents.

Dating back to classical rhetoric formulated by Aristotle—categorised into *ethos* (authority and character), *pathos* (emotional resonance), and *logos* (logical argumentation)—the analysis of political oratory decodes how language, metaphor, narrative framing, and staging work collectively to generate consent and legitimacy.

## Why it matters
Political speeches are among the most consequential public communications in civic life. They announce declarations of war, proclaim civil rights milestones, define fiscal and legislative agendas, and set the moral tone of governance. Understanding how speeches work shields citizens from demagoguery and emotional manipulation.

By deconstructing the rhetoric of leaders, citizens can distinguish substantive policy commitments from hollow slogans, evaluate the logical coherence of proposed reforms, and identify which social groups are privileged or excluded by the speaker's narrative.

## How it works
Analysing a political address involves scrutinizing several complementary layers:

1. **Context and Audience**: Identifying the primary audience (the room) and secondary audiences (television viewers, domestic voters, international adversaries, financial markets). Speeches often contain coded appeals—dog whistles—intended to communicate specific meanings to selected demographic subgroups while sounding innocuous to the broader public.
2. **Classical Rhetorical Appeals**:
   - *Ethos*: How the speaker establishes personal integrity, shared values, and authority to lead.
   - *Pathos*: Emotional storytelling, invocations of national pride, or calculated fear appeals designed to disarm critical resistance.
   - *Logos*: The empirical evidence, policy arguments, and causal links presented to justify actions.
3. **Metaphor and Conceptual Framing**: Examining the metaphorical structures used to frame issues. Framing a social problem as a "war" (e.g., the War on Drugs, the War on Poverty) justifies emergency measures, top-down command, and calls for uncritical unity.
4. **Binary Oppositions and Scapegoating**: Analyzing how the speech constructs the "in-group" (the virtuous people, patriots) versus the "out-group" (corrupt elites, subversives, foreign enemies).
5. **Omissions and Unstated Assumptions**: Identifying what is left unsaid. What critical counterarguments, historical facts, or affected communities are entirely erased from the narrative?

## Contested points
The evaluation of political rhetoric generates persistent disagreement regarding authenticity and democratic deliberation.

One debate concerns the tension between rhetorical eloquence and policy substance. Modern speechwriting is heavily professionalised; political addresses are drafted by teams of specialists, vetted by pollsters, and tested on focus groups. Critics argue that this produces glossy, focus-grouped rhetoric designed to project empathy without committing to concrete structural redistribution, masking technocratic governance behind stirring moral language. Conversely, defenders of political rhetoric argue that inspiring oratory is essential in a democracy to unite diverse coalitions and mobilise public enthusiasm for difficult sacrifices.

Another controversy concerns populist oratory versus institutional technocracy. Populist leaders often reject formal diplomatic and legalistic speech conventions, adopting colloquial, confrontational, and anti-elitist language. While institutionalists criticize this as demagoguery that degrades civic norms, supporters view it as authentic, unfiltered communication that dismantles hypocritical elite etiquette.

## Examples
A celebrated example of democratic oratory is Abraham Lincoln's Gettysburg Address (1863). In fewer than 300 words, Lincoln reframed the American Civil War not merely as a constitutional struggle to preserve the Union, but as a moral test of whether a nation "dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal" could endure, defining democratic governance as "government of the people, by the people, for the people."

In contrast, George W. Bush's January 2002 State of the Union address demonstrated the strategic use of rhetorical framing. By grouping Iran, Iraq, and North Korea under the phrase "Axis of Evil," the speech constructed a unified existential adversary, rhetorically laying the groundwork for preventive military intervention in Iraq in 2003.

In Iran, political speeches by the Supreme Leader and state officials are highly ritualised ideological performances. Addresses delivered at Friday prayers or state anniversaries consistently deploy binary oppositions contrasting "the pious, steadfast Iranian nation" with "global arrogance" (استکبار جهانی) led by the United States. Speeches frame domestic economic hardships not as failures of state economic policy, but as part of an ongoing "hybrid war" (جنگ ترکیبی) waged by foreign adversaries, delegitimising domestic protests as foreign-orchestrated sedition.
