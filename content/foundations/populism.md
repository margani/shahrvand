## Definition

Populism is a distinct political logic and "thin-centered ideology" that frames society as fundamentally divided into two antagonistic, morally opposed groups: "the pure people" versus "the corrupt elite" (Mudde, 2004). Populist leaders argue that politics should be an unmediated expression of the general will of the ordinary people, whom they claim exclusively to represent. Because it lacks a comprehensive economic or social philosophy on its own, populism attaches itself as a host framework to either left-wing ideologies (targeting corporate oligarchs and finance capital) or right-wing ideologies (targeting cultural establishments, globalist bureaucrats, and immigrant groups).

## Why it matters

Populism has transformed political competition in established and emerging democracies worldwide. Populist movements frequently emerge when established political parties fail to address widespread economic anxieties, cultural alienation, or perceived democratic deficits. While populism can bring neglected concerns to the forefront and revitalize political participation, it frequently challenges constitutional [checks and balances](../institutions/separation-of-powers), weakens independent institutions like the [judiciary](../institutions/judiciary-and-courts), and rejects political compromise by casting opponents as enemies of the people.

## How it works

### Core conceptual elements

Political scientists identify three central features defining populist politics (Mudde and Rovira Kaltwasser, 2017; Müller, 2016):

- **The pure people**: romanticized as a virtuous, authentic, and homogeneous community possessing innate common sense, whose collective interests are portrayed as singular and self-evident.
- **The corrupt elite**: depicted as self-serving, out-of-touch politicians, financiers, academics, and media figures who have betrayed the national community for personal gain.
- **Monopolistic moral representation**: populist actors claim not merely to represent *some* citizens, but to be the *only* authentic voice of *all* genuine people. Jan-Werner Müller emphasizes this anti-pluralist stance: populists cast political competitors as illegitimate traitors rather than legitimate democratic rivals (Müller, 2016).

### Left vs. Right Populism

- **Left-wing populism**: defines the division along socioeconomic lines: the working masses versus the billionaire class, large banks, and multinational corporations. Common in Latin America (e.g., Hugo Chávez in Venezuela) and parts of southern Europe (e.g., Podemos in Spain, Syriza in Greece).
- **Right-wing populism**: defines the division along cultural and national lines: native citizens versus cosmopolitan globalists and foreign migrants. It emphasizes national sovereignty, border enforcement, and cultural defense (e.g., radical right parties in Europe and North America).

## Contested points

- **Democratic renewal vs. Authoritarian erosion**: defenders view populism as a corrective to oligarchic stagnation, giving voice to marginalized citizens whom technocratic elites ignored. Critics argue populism poses an existential threat to [liberal democracy](political-regimes) because it delegitimizes minority rights, dismisses institutional constraints on executive power, and fosters personalized autocracy (Levitsky and Ziblatt, 2018).
- **Who are "the people"?** Political pluralists argue that "the people" is a diverse mosaic of competing groups, values, and interests with no single unified will. Populism's assumption of a single moral people inevitably leads to the exclusion of anyone who dissents from the leader's agenda.
- **Style vs. Ideology**: scholars debate whether populism is an ideology (Mudde, 2004) or primarily an opportunistic political communication style and rhetorical strategy used by charismatic leaders to mobilize grievance.

## Examples

- **United States, late 19th century**: the People's Party (Populists) of the 1890s organized small farmers and industrial workers against railroad monopolies, eastern banks, and political bosses, pioneering modern populist campaigning and demanding progressive reforms.
- **Latin America (Peronism in Argentina)**: Juan Domingo Perón combined charismatic leadership, mass labor mobilization (*los descamisados*), nationalist industrialization, and anti-imperialist rhetoric in the 1940s and 1950s, creating an enduring populist political movement.
- **Iran, 2005–2013**: the presidency of Mahmoud Ahmadinejad exemplified populist politics, deploying simple colloquial language, visiting impoverished rural provinces, and contrasting the righteous piety of ordinary *mostazafan* (the disinherited) with alleged corruption among entrenched political elites and clerics.
