## Definition

Legitimacy is the widespread belief that a political authority — a government, a law, an institution — has the right to govern and that its decisions ought to be obeyed. It is the quality that turns raw [power](power-and-authority) into accepted rule. A government that has legitimacy can govern largely through consent rather than coercion; one that lacks it must rely increasingly on force.

## Why it matters

Legitimacy determines whether people cooperate with or resist the authorities they live under. High legitimacy lowers the cost of governing: citizens pay taxes, follow laws, and serve in the military without constant compulsion. When legitimacy erodes, the cost of maintaining order rises — through surveillance, censorship, corruption, or violence — and the risk of protest, revolution, or state collapse grows. Understanding legitimacy is therefore essential to understanding political stability and change.

## How it works

### Sources of legitimacy

Legitimacy can rest on different foundations. Building on Max Weber's typology (see [Power and authority](power-and-authority)), political scientists identify several common sources:

- **Performance legitimacy**: the government delivers economic growth, security, or public services. Some scholars argue that the Chinese Communist Party's post-1978 governance relies heavily on performance legitimacy (Zhao, 2009).
- **Democratic legitimacy**: the government is chosen by the people through free and fair [elections](../institutions/elections-and-voting-systems) and respects the [rule of law](../rights/rights-and-rule-of-law). Article 21 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) states: "The will of the people shall be the basis of the authority of government."
- **Religious or ideological legitimacy**: the government derives authority from a religious mandate or an ideological vision. The concept of *velayat-e faqih* (ولایت فقیه, guardianship of the jurist) in Iran's 1979 constitution grounds the Supreme Leader's authority in Shia Islamic jurisprudence.
- **Traditional legitimacy**: the government rests on long-standing custom, as in hereditary monarchies (see Weber's typology in [Power and authority](power-and-authority)).
- **Legal legitimacy**: authority derives from following established constitutional and legal procedures — a [constitution](../institutions/constitutions) that defines how power is acquired and exercised.

In practice, most governments draw on more than one source at once.

### How legitimacy is lost

Legitimacy is not permanent. Common paths to losing it include:

- **Corruption and self-dealing** that undermine the government's claim to serve the public.
- **Repression** that is widely seen as disproportionate or unjust.
- **Economic failure** that destroys performance legitimacy.
- **Electoral fraud** that discredits democratic legitimacy.
- **A gap between stated principles and practice** — for example, a government that claims democratic legitimacy but suppresses opposition.

## Contested points

- **Can authoritarian regimes be legitimate?** This is one of the most debated questions in political science. Some scholars argue that legitimacy requires democratic consent; others contend that regimes may earn genuine popular acceptance through performance, ideology, or tradition, even without competitive elections. The key factual point: the UDHR and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR, 1966, Article 25) establish a legal norm that the basis of government authority is the will of the people expressed through periodic and genuine elections. Whether this norm is universally accepted as a moral principle remains debated.
- **Who decides legitimacy — the people or external observers?** Some theorists define legitimacy descriptively (the population believes the government is rightful) while others define it normatively (the government meets certain ethical standards whether or not people believe it does). Both usages appear in political writing, and confusing them is a common source of misunderstanding.

## Examples

- **United Kingdom**: the monarchy's legitimacy has evolved from divine right (medieval period) to constitutional and ceremonial tradition (after the Glorious Revolution of 1688), resting today on custom, legal authority, and popular acceptance rather than active political power.
- **Iran**: the Islamic Republic's legitimacy claims combine religious authority (the doctrine of *velayat-e faqih*), revolutionary charisma, and republican institutions (elections for president and parliament). Critics within and outside Iran dispute whether the system's restrictions on candidates and civil liberties undermine its democratic legitimacy.
- **European Union**: the EU faces an ongoing "democratic deficit" debate. Critics argue its institutions, especially the European Commission, lack direct democratic legitimacy because commissioners are appointed rather than elected. Defenders point to the directly elected European Parliament and the democratic mandate of member-state governments that sit in the Council.
