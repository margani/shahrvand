## Definition

Conservatism is a political philosophy that emphasizes the preservation of established traditions, social order, and time-tested institutions such as the family, community associations, constitutional conventions, and property rights. Emerging in opposition to the radical upheavals of the French Revolution, conservatism views human society not as an abstract machine that can be engineered from scratch, but as an organic, fragile partnership between generations. Conservatives advocate prudent, incremental reform over revolutionary rupture.

## Why it matters

Conservatism has been one of the primary governing philosophies across democratic nations, shaping market policies, legal doctrines, judicial restraint, and civic institutions. Understanding conservative thought is crucial for comprehending debates over constitutional stability, national defense, criminal justice, fiscal prudence, and cultural continuity.

## How it works

### Core conservative principles

The conservative intellectual tradition is structured around several central themes (Burke, 1790; Oakeshott, 1962):

- **Tradition and accumulated wisdom**: respect for practices, laws, and customs that have evolved over centuries, reflecting the collective wisdom of past generations rather than the untested theories of intellectuals.
- **Human imperfection**: the view that human nature is inherently flawed, driven by passion as well as reason. Consequently, external institutions (laws, religion, social morals) are necessary to restrain disorder and crime.
- **Organic society**: society is seen not as a loose collection of atomized individuals bound merely by commercial contracts, but as a living organism with interdependent parts, duties, and reciprocal bonds.
- **Hierarchy and authority**: acceptance that natural differences in talent and leadership produce social hierarchy, which functions constructively when accompanied by civic responsibility (*noblesse oblige*).
- **Pragmatism over dogma**: suspicion of rigid ideological blueprints, preferring practical experience, precedent, and careful observation (Oakeshott, 1962).

### Major streams of conservatism

- **Traditional (One Nation) conservatism**: associated with British statesman Edmund Burke and 19th-century Prime Minister Benjamin Disraeli. It stresses social cohesion, aristocratic responsibility toward the working poor, and state intervention to maintain national unity.
- **Neoconservatism**: developed in the late 20th century, emphasizing an assertive foreign policy, democratic promotion abroad, and cultural traditionalism at home.
- **Fiscal (libertarian) conservatism**: emphasizes free markets, deregulation, low public debt, and minimal government intervention in the economy, closely converging with classical liberalism.

## Contested points

- **Preservation vs. social justice**: critics argue that conservative defense of "tradition" often serves to justify inherited inequalities, institutional racism, and outdated social privileges. Conservatives reply that dismantling imperfect institutions without proven alternatives leads to chaos and tyranny, as demonstrated by radical revolutions.
- **Resistance to change**: while critics characterize conservatism as mere reactionary nostalgia, defenders (such as Burke) emphasize that "a state without the means of some change is without the means of its conservation." The conservative goal is not immobility, but reform that preserves institutional stability.
- **Individualism vs. social order**: a major internal fissure exists between free-market conservatives who champion deregulation and individual enterprise, and cultural traditionalists who worry that uninhibited consumer capitalism destroys local communities, families, and moral traditions.

## Examples

- **Edmund Burke's critique of the French Revolution (1790)**: Burke predicted that tearing down France's monarchy, church, and legal traditions based on abstract universal rights would culminate in terror and military dictatorship, establishing the foundational text of modern conservative theory.
- **United States Constitution (1787)**: the institutional design of the U.S. Constitution — with its staggered Senate terms, presidential veto, and supermajority amendment hurdles — reflects conservative skepticism of direct popular passions, creating mechanisms for deliberation and gradual change.
- **Iran**: conservative thought in modern Iran has historically manifested in the defense of Islamic jurisprudence (Sharia) and religious institutions against secular Westernization, from Sheikh Fazlollah Nouri's opposition to secular constitutionalists in 1907 to contemporary Principlist (*Osul-gara*) political factions advocating religious tradition, anti-imperialism, and clerical leadership.
