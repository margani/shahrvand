## Definition

Liberalism is a foundational political tradition that places the individual and individual liberty at the core of political life. Emerging in Europe during the Enlightenment, liberalism argues that human beings are endowed with fundamental rights that predate governments, and that political [authority](power-and-authority) is legitimate only when based on the consent of the governed. While liberals agree on core values such as constitutional limits on power, tolerance, and the [rule of law](../rights/rights-and-rule-of-law), they diverge significantly regarding the appropriate economic role of the [state](the-state).

## Why it matters

Liberal ideas underpin modern constitutional democracy, international human rights law (such as the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948), and global market economies. Principles commonly taken for granted in contemporary democracies — freedom of speech, freedom of religion, trial by jury, representative government, and property rights — are direct contributions of the liberal philosophical tradition.

## How it works

### Core philosophical principles

Despite internal variations, liberal thought is organized around several recurring pillars (Locke, 1689; Mill, 1859; Rawls, 1971):

- **Individualism**: the conviction that the individual human being has primary moral significance, superior to the claims of social collectives, tribes, or states.
- **Freedom and autonomy**: liberty to act, speak, believe, and worship without arbitrary interference, famously articulated in J.S. Mill's "harm principle" — that power can only be rightfully exercised over any individual against their will to prevent harm to others (Mill, 1859).
- **Reason and progress**: faith in human rational capability to solve social problems through debate, inquiry, and scientific discovery rather than blind adherence to dogma or custom.
- **Constitutionalism and consent**: power must be constrained through [constitutions](../institutions/constitutions) and institutional [checks and balances](../institutions/separation-of-powers).
- **Toleration**: accepting moral, religious, and political pluralism as normal and beneficial in a free society.

### Classical vs. Modern (Social) Liberalism

Historically, liberalism bifurcated into two major streams regarding economic justice and state power:

- **Classical liberalism**: developed in the 18th and 19th centuries (John Locke, Adam Smith). It favors a "night-watchman state" (strictly limited government confined to defense, police, and contracts), free markets, low taxation, and negative liberty (freedom from state interference).
- **Modern (social) liberalism**: developed in the late 19th and 20th centuries (John Stuart Mill, John Maynard Keynes, John Rawls). It argues that unrestricted markets can produce extreme poverty, monopolies, and social exclusion, denying disadvantaged citizens the genuine ability to exercise their freedom. Social liberals advocate positive liberty — empowering citizens through state-funded public education, healthcare, and social safety nets.

## Contested points

- **Atomism vs. community**: critics from conservative, communitarian, and socialist perspectives argue that liberalism's intense focus on individual autonomy fosters a self-centered society that dissolves family ties, cultural solidarity, and shared civic duties (Sandel, 1982). Liberals reply that individual freedom enables citizens to choose voluntary communities freely without coercion.
- **Property rights vs. social equality**: classical liberals view private property as an essential barrier protecting individuals from state tyranny. Egalitarians and socialists counter that unfettered private ownership allows wealth concentration that undermines political democracy.
- **Universalism vs. cultural imperialism**: post-colonial and non-Western critics argue that liberal individualist values reflect specific Western historical experiences and should not be imposed universally. Liberal philosophers counter that the desire for basic bodily autonomy, freedom from torture, and protection against state violence is universal.

## Examples

- **England, 1689**: the Glorious Revolution and the enactment of the English Bill of Rights established parliamentary supremacy and constitutional constraints on monarchy, heavily influencing John Locke's *Two Treatises of Government*.
- **United States, 1776**: the Declaration of Independence incorporated Lockean liberal principles, asserting that all individuals possess inalienable rights to "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness," and that governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed.
- **Iran, 1906**: the intellectuals and merchants of the Iranian Constitutional Revolution drew heavily on liberal constitutional concepts to establish the Majles and draft the first Fundamental Law, curbing absolute royal despotism (*estebdad*).
