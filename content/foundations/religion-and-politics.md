## Definition

Religion and politics addresses the complex relationship between systems of sacred faith, ecclesiastical institutions, and the exercise of political [power](power-and-authority) within human societies. Across history, religion has served both to legitimize state authority (through divine mandates and royal consecrations) and to inspire radical movements against tyranny and social injustice. In modern political science, this dynamic centers on how [states](the-state) manage religious diversity, the role of faith-based moral arguments in public lawmaking, and the spectrum of institutional arrangements ranging from theocracies to strictly secular republics.

## Why it matters

Contrary to 20th-century secularization theories predicting the inevitable retreat of religion from public affairs, religious convictions remain among the most potent forces mobilizing citizens, defining national identities, and driving geopolitical alliances today. Debates over family law, reproductive rights, education curricula, minority freedoms, and international conflict continuously intersect with religious beliefs across both democracies and autocracies.

## How it works

### Institutional models of Church-State relations

States structure their institutional relationships with religious traditions through distinct constitutional frameworks (Fox, 2008):

- **Theocracy**: a system in which political power is held by religious authorities or where supreme civil authority is claimed to derive directly from divine law, with laws interpreted by religious jurists (e.g., the Vatican City; the Islamic Republic of Iran).
- **Established (State) religion**: the state grants official constitutional recognition and symbolic or financial privileges to a specific denomination, while generally guaranteeing freedom of worship to religious minorities (e.g., the Church of England in the United Kingdom; Lutheranism in Denmark).
- **Separation of church and state (Secularism)**: the state maintains institutional neutrality toward all faiths, neither endorsing nor funding religion, guaranteeing individual freedom of conscience and worship while precluding religious doctrine from dictating statutory law (e.g., the First Amendment of the U.S. Constitution; French *laïcité*).
- **State anti-clericalism or state atheism**: the state actively suppresses religious institutions, promotes secularist or atheist ideologies, and restricts religious practice from the public sphere (e.g., the Soviet Union; revolutionary France in 1793).

### Religion as a political force

Sociologists and political scientists analyze religion's dual political capacity (Casanova, 1994):
- **A source of legitimation**: ruling regimes frequently deploy sacred symbols, scriptures, and clerical alliances to demand obedience and sanctify state authority (see [Legitimacy](legitimacy)).
- **A source of liberation and prophetic resistance**: religious communities frequently act as autonomous civil spaces resisting authoritarian oppression, invoking divine moral accountability against worldly despots.

## Contested points

- **Public reason vs. Religious expression**: political philosopher John Rawls argued that in pluralist democracies, coercive laws must be justified through "public reason" — arguments accessible to all citizens regardless of their religious faith (Rawls, 1993). Critics counter that demanding religious citizens bracket their deepest moral convictions in public debate is unfair and exclusionary, arguing that democratic dialogue should accommodate faith-based motivations openly.
- **Religious freedom vs. Non-discrimination**: in contemporary legal disputes, claims of religious freedom (e.g., exemptions from providing certain services, dress codes, employment mandates) frequently clash with state laws protecting gender equality and non-discrimination.
- **The secularization debate**: sociologists historically assumed modernization inevitably leads to secularization (declining religious belief and practice). Contemporary scholars emphasize that while secularization occurred in Western Europe, religion has experienced dynamic revival and political resurgence across the Global South, the Middle East, and parts of the United States.

## Examples

- **The American Civil Rights Movement (1950s–1960s)**: led by ministers like the Reverend Martin Luther King Jr., the movement drew rhetorical power, moral philosophy, and community mobilization directly from Black Christian churches to challenge racial apartheid non-violently.
- **Poland, 1978–1989**: the election of Pope John Paul II and the moral backing of the Catholic Church provided essential spiritual and institutional protection for the *Solidarność* (Solidarity) movement against the communist state.
- **Iran**: the relationship between Shia Islam and the state has evolved dramatically: from clerical participation in the 1906 Constitutional Revolution (allied with merchants and intellectuals), to confrontation with Pahlavi secular modernization, and culminating in the 1979 Revolution. Under the leadership of Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini, the 1979 constitution established the doctrine of *velayat-e faqih* (guardianship of the jurist), integrating Shia jurisprudence into the core of constitutional governance.
