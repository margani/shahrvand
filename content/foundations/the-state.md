## Definition

The state is a political entity that claims sovereign authority over a defined territory and its population. The most widely cited legal definition comes from the Montevideo Convention on the Rights and Duties of States (1933), which lists four criteria: a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states (Article 1). In everyday language, "state" and "country" are often used interchangeably, but in political science "the state" refers specifically to the institutions that exercise [authority](power-and-authority) — the government, courts, military, police, and bureaucracy — not to the people or the land alone.

## Why it matters

The state is the main framework through which political power is organised today. Almost every person on Earth lives under the jurisdiction of one or more states. States make and enforce laws, collect taxes, provide public services, wage war, and represent their people internationally. Understanding what a state is and how it differs from a nation, a government, or a regime is essential for making sense of political news.

**Key distinctions:**

- **State vs. government**: the state is the permanent institutional structure; the government is the group of people who run it at a given time. Governments change; the state endures.
- **State vs. nation**: a nation is a group of people who share a common identity (language, culture, history). A nation-state is when the two roughly overlap (e.g. Japan), but many states contain multiple nations (e.g. India, Nigeria), and some nations lack a state (e.g. the Kurds).

## How it works

### Sovereignty

The defining feature of a state is sovereignty: supreme authority within its territory and independence from external control. Sovereignty has two faces:

- **Internal sovereignty**: the state's authority over everyone within its borders. The sociologist Max Weber defined the state as the entity that "successfully claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory" (Weber, 1919).
- **External sovereignty**: recognition by other states that this entity governs its territory independently. The United Nations system is built on this principle (UN Charter, Article 2).

### How states form

States have arisen through conquest, colonisation, revolution, negotiation, or the breakup of empires. There is no single path. Some recent examples: South Sudan became a state by referendum in 2011; the reunification of Germany in 1990 merged two states into one; the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991 produced fifteen successor states.

## Contested points

- **Is the state necessary?** Anarchists argue that the state is inherently coercive and should be replaced by voluntary association. Socialists in the Marxist tradition have viewed the state as a tool of class domination, though they disagree on whether it should be seized and transformed or abolished. Liberals and conservatives generally accept the state as necessary but differ on how large and active it should be.
- **Failed states**: when a government loses effective control of its territory, the entity may be described as a "failed state." This label is contested: critics argue it reflects the perspective of powerful states and ignores why institutions collapsed, often due to colonialism or external intervention.
- **Whose sovereignty?** The concept of state sovereignty can conflict with human rights. The doctrine of "Responsibility to Protect" (R2P), endorsed by the UN General Assembly in 2005, holds that sovereignty entails a responsibility to protect populations from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, and crimes against humanity — and that the international community may act when a state manifestly fails to do so (UN General Assembly Resolution 60/1, 2005).

## Examples

- **Westphalia, 1648**: the Peace of Westphalia, ending the Thirty Years' War in Europe, is widely cited as the origin of the modern state system based on territorial sovereignty, though historians debate how sharply it broke with earlier arrangements (Osiander, 2001).
- **Iran**: Iran illustrates continuity and change. It has existed as a political entity for millennia. The modern Iranian state was reshaped by the Constitutional Revolution (1906), the Pahlavi centralisation (1920s–1970s), and the Islamic Revolution (1979), which created the current dual structure of elected institutions and clerical oversight.
- **India, 1947**: the partition of British India created two new states, India and Pakistan, along broadly religious lines, demonstrating that state formation often involves conflict and displacement.
