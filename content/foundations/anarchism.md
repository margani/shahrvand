## Definition

Anarchism is a political philosophy and social movement centered on the rejection of the [state](the-state) and all compulsory, hierarchical authority. Deriving from the ancient Greek *anarkhia* (meaning "without a ruler"), anarchism argues that the state is inherently coercive, exploitative, and destructive of human freedom. In place of governmental coercion, anarchists propose organizing human society through decentralized, voluntary associations, direct democracy, and cooperative networks without institutional rulers.

## Why it matters

Although rarely adopted as the official governing framework of a nation-state, anarchism has exerted a profound influence on modern political history. It shaped the 19th-century international labor movement, pioneered radical critiques of state bureaucracy and centralized capitalism, and provided organizational models (horizontalism, affinity groups, consensus decision-making) adopted by contemporary anti-globalization, environmental, and civil rights protests.

## How it works

### Core theoretical tenets

Despite internal variations, anarchist theory is unified by several foundational commitments (Proudhon, 1840; Bakunin, 1873; Kropotkin, 1902):

- **Anti-statism**: the conviction that the state possesses no legitimate authority; its laws are backed by violence, and its institutions exist to protect the privileges of ruling classes. Pierre-Joseph Proudhon famously declared, "To be governed is to be watched, inspected, spied upon, directed, law-driven... and fleeced" (Proudhon, 1851).
- **Voluntary association**: human social order should emerge from freely negotiated contracts and mutual agreements between individuals and self-governing local communes, not state mandates.
- **Mutual aid**: Peter Kropotkin argued in *Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution* (1902) that evolutionary biology shows cooperation and mutual support, rather than violent competition, are the primary drivers of animal and human societal flourishing.
- **Direct action**: rejecting participation in parliamentary [elections](../institutions/elections-and-voting-systems) and conventional political parties, anarchists advocate direct action — strikes, boycotts, civil disobedience, and alternative cooperative institutions — to dismantle oppression directly.

### Major schools of anarchist thought

- **Collectivist and Communist Anarchism**: associated with Mikhail Bakunin and Peter Kropotkin. It advocates the complete abolition of both the state and private wage labor, replacing them with common ownership of productive resources, decentralized federations of communes, and distribution according to the principle "from each according to ability, to each according to need."
- **Mutualism**: pioneered by Proudhon, envisioning a market system of independent artisans and worker-owned cooperatives trading products at cost, funded by interest-free mutual credit banks.
- **Anarcho-Syndicalism**: focuses on the industrial working class, proposing that revolutionary trade unions (syndicates) should seize factories and manage the economy directly through workers' councils.
- **Individualist Anarchism and Anarcho-Capitalism**: individualist thinkers prioritize absolute personal autonomy; anarcho-capitalists propose privatizing all state functions (including courts and policing) within a pure market economy, an approach traditional social anarchists reject as incompatible with anti-hierarchical principles.

## Contested points

- **Order without the state**: the central objection raised by liberals and conservatives (Hobbes, 1651) is that without a centralized state to enforce law and contracts, society would descend into violent disorder, criminal predation, and chaos. Anarchists respond that violence and crime are overwhelmingly products of economic inequality and state oppression, and that voluntary communities can maintain social order through peer accountability and restorative justice.
- **Scalability and modern complexity**: critics argue that face-to-face consensus and decentralized communes cannot manage large-scale modern infrastructure, such as national healthcare systems, air traffic networks, or global pandemic responses.
- **Means and ends: Violence vs. Pacifism**: in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, some anarchists embraced "propaganda of the deed" (assassinations of monarchs and heads of state). Most contemporary anarchists repudiate terrorism, emphasizing non-violent direct action, community mutual aid, and prefigurative politics — creating egalitarian social spaces in the present.

## Examples

- **The Paris Commune, 1871**: during the Franco-Prussian War, citizens of Paris established a radical self-governing municipal council with worker-managed enterprises and recallable delegates, serving as an inspiring historical model for both anarchists and socialists.
- **Revolutionary Spain, 1936–1939**: during the Spanish Civil War, the anarcho-syndicalist union CNT organized vast swathes of Catalonia and Aragon, collectivizing agricultural land, running public transportation, and fighting fascism without a centralized state apparatus (Orwell, 1938).
- **Rojava (Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria)**: influenced by Murray Bookchin's libertarian municipalism and "democratic confederalism," Kurdish and allied communities developed a decentralized governance system based on local neighborhood communes and gender equality.
