## Definition

Political culture comprises the deeply rooted pattern of orientations, psychological values, beliefs, and emotional sentiments that members of a society hold toward their political system, its institutions, and their own role as political actors. Coined in its modern scientific sense by Gabriel Almond and Sidney Verba in *The Civic Culture* (1963), the concept explains that formal constitutional structures (such as parliaments, courts, and voting laws) do not operate in a vacuum; they function effectively only when supported by congruent societal attitudes toward authority, trust, tolerance, and civic participation.

## Why it matters

Identical formal constitutions produce radically different outcomes in different societies. When democratic constitutions were transplanted into newly decolonized states in the mid-20th century or post-communist societies in the 1990s, some developed stable democracies while others descended into autocracy or civil conflict. Understanding political culture helps explain why legal rules alone cannot guarantee stable democracy without a supporting culture of mutual trust, institutional legitimacy, and civic responsibility.

## How it works

### Almond and Verba's three ideal types

In their pioneering comparative study across five nations, Almond and Verba identified three baseline types of political culture (Almond and Verba, 1963):

- **Parochial culture**: citizens have minimal awareness of or expectations from the central government; political life is localized around tribe, clan, or village, with virtually no expectation of citizen influence.
- **Subject culture**: citizens are aware of the central government, its laws, and its police, but view themselves strictly as passive subjects who obey directives rather than active participants who shape policy.
- **Participant culture**: citizens are highly aware of political institutions, believe they have the capacity to influence political decisions (high internal efficacy), and actively engage through voting, campaigning, and community organizing.

Almond and Verba concluded that the most stable democratic order is a balanced "civic culture" — a composite blend combining high participant activism with enough subject-culture respect for law and order to maintain governmental stability.

### Social capital and political trust

In *Making Democracy Work* (1993), Robert Putnam demonstrated through twenty years of empirical research in Italy that regional differences in institutional performance were driven by "social capital" — networks of civic engagement, interpersonal trust, and norms of reciprocity cultivated in choral societies, soccer clubs, and cooperative associations. Where social capital was rich, democratic institutions governed effectively and clean public administration thrived; where social capital was scarce, politics was dominated by cynical clientelism, corruption, and mafia coercion.

## Contested points

- **Culture as cause vs. consequence**: does a participatory political culture create stable democratic institutions, or do well-designed, fair institutions gradually generate a democratic civic culture? Institutionalist political scientists argue that people adapt their behavior to institutional incentives: fair courts create social trust, while corrupt courts breed cynicism. Modern political science views the relationship as reciprocal.
- **Cultural determinism and stereotyping**: critics argue that political culture analyses risk degenerating into essentialist cultural stereotyping, such as claiming that certain religious or cultural traditions (e.g., Confucianism or Islam) are "inherently incompatible" with democracy. Scholars point out that societies are internally diverse and cultures evolve dynamically over time, as demonstrated by the democratization of Catholic Europe and East Asia.
- **Culture wars and polarization**: in contemporary democracies, political culture is increasingly fragmented along partisan and cultural lines, where rival camps hold diametrically opposed orientations toward truth, scientific authority, electoral legitimacy, and national identity (Inglehart and Welzel, 2005).

## Examples

- **United States and Great Britain (1960s study)**: identified by Almond and Verba as approximating the "civic culture," where widespread citizen participation was balanced by broad underlying trust in constitutional conventions and peaceful transfer of executive power.
- **Weimar Republic, Germany (1919–1933)**: had one of the most progressive democratic constitutions of its era, but collapsed into totalitarian fascism largely because large segments of the judiciary, military, aristocracy, and public remained wedded to an authoritarian subject culture deeply hostile to parliamentary democracy.
- **Iran**: political scientists describe Iran's political culture as a complex, multifaceted arena shaped by centuries of autocratic royal rule (*estebdad*), deep Shia traditions of martyrdom and anti-tyranny resistance, profound national pride, and persistent skepticism of state transparency. Despite legal and security constraints, Iranian society demonstrates high levels of informal political consciousness, widespread voter participation in decisive electoral moments, and recurring civic mobilization for rights and rule of law.
