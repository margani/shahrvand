## Definition

Power is the ability to influence or control the behaviour of others, even against their will. Authority is power that people accept as legitimate — that is, they obey not just because they are forced but because they believe the person or institution has the right to command. The distinction matters because governments that rely on power alone (coercion) tend to be unstable, while those that also enjoy authority can govern with less force.

## Why it matters

Understanding who holds power — and whether that power is seen as legitimate — helps explain why some governments are obeyed voluntarily while others must rely on surveillance, censorship, or violence. It also explains why revolutions happen: when enough people stop recognising a ruler's authority, coercion alone may not be enough to maintain control. (See [Legitimacy](legitimacy).)

## How it works

### Types of power

Political scientists identify several forms of power:

- **Coercive power**: the threat or use of force. A government that arrests dissidents exercises coercive power.
- **Economic power**: control over resources, jobs, or money. Sanctions between states are an exercise of economic power.
- **Soft power**: influence through culture, values, or ideas rather than force. Joseph Nye coined this term in 1990 to describe how a country's culture and political ideals can attract others without coercion (Nye, 1990).
- **Structural power**: the ability to shape the framework within which others operate, such as setting the rules of an international trade system.

### Weber's three types of authority

The sociologist Max Weber (1864–1920) proposed three ideal types of legitimate authority, still widely used in political science:

1. **Traditional authority**: resting on long-established customs and the belief that certain institutions have always had the right to rule, such as hereditary monarchies.
2. **Charismatic authority**: resting on the personal qualities of a leader — their heroism, vision, or perceived sanctity. This type is inherently unstable because it depends on one individual.
3. **Rational-legal authority**: resting on established laws and procedures. A president or prime minister has authority because they were selected through constitutional rules, not because of personal charisma or ancient custom. Most modern [states](the-state) claim this type.

Weber emphasised that these are ideal types; real governments usually combine elements of more than one (Weber, 1922).

## Contested points

- **Is all power ultimately coercion?** Some political realists argue that behind every law stands the threat of force. Others, such as Hannah Arendt, argued that power arises from collective agreement and is distinct from violence: "Power corresponds to the human ability not just to act but to act in concert" (Arendt, 1970).
- **Can authority exist without democracy?** Defenders of authoritarian rule argue that stability, order, or religious mandate can confer legitimate authority even without elections. Democratic theorists counter that lasting legitimacy requires the consent of the governed. This site does not take a side but notes that international human-rights law, including the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), Article 21, recognises the will of the people as the basis of governmental authority.

## Examples

- **France, 1789**: The French Revolution illustrates a collapse of traditional authority. Louis XVI's claim to rule by divine right lost its hold as economic crisis and new ideas about popular sovereignty spread.
- **Iran, 1979**: The revolution replaced the Pahlavi monarchy (a mix of traditional and rational-legal authority) with the Islamic Republic, in which Ayatollah Khomeini exercised charismatic and religious authority. The post-revolutionary constitution created institutions of rational-legal authority (a president, a parliament) alongside the position of Supreme Leader (ولی فقیه), which draws on religious authority.
- **South Africa, 1990–1994**: Nelson Mandela's moral authority, earned through decades of resistance and imprisonment, was widely credited with enabling the peaceful transition from apartheid to democracy.
