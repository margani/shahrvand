## Definition

Politics is the process by which groups of people make collective decisions, distribute resources, and settle disagreements about how to live together. It happens wherever people must agree on shared rules — in a country, a city council, a workplace, or an international body. The word comes from the Greek *polis*, meaning city-state, reflecting its origins in the public life of ancient Athens.

## Why it matters

Every person is affected by political decisions, whether or not they participate in making them. Tax rates, school curricula, speed limits, healthcare systems, and criminal laws are all products of politics. Understanding how these decisions are made — and who makes them — is a first step toward informed citizenship.

## How it works

Political scientists describe politics through several overlapping lenses:

- **Power**: who can get others to act, and how. (See [Power and authority](power-and-authority).)
- **Institutions**: the organisations and rules that structure decision-making, such as parliaments, courts, and constitutions. (See [The state](the-state) and [Constitutions](../institutions/constitutions).)
- **Ideas**: the ideologies and values — liberalism, conservatism, socialism, Islamism, nationalism, and others — that shape what people want politics to do.
- **Conflict and cooperation**: politics arises because people disagree, and it provides ways (from debate to coercion) to handle disagreement.

At its most basic, politics answers three questions: Who decides? By what rules? And who benefits?

## Contested points

There is no single agreed definition of politics. Two prominent views illustrate the range:

- **Politics as the art of government.** On this view, politics is what governments and state officials do: legislating, taxing, waging war, conducting diplomacy. This is the older, narrower meaning, found in Aristotle's *Politics* (c. 350 BCE), where he called the human being a "political animal" because people naturally live in organised communities.
- **Politics as power relations everywhere.** A broader view, associated with the political scientist Harold Lasswell, defines politics as "who gets what, when, how" (Lasswell, 1936). Feminist scholars extended this further: Kate Millett argued in *Sexual Politics* (1970) that power relations in families and workplaces are also political.

Neither view is "correct" in a factual sense; each highlights different aspects of collective life. This site uses the broader sense — decisions about shared rules, wherever they occur — while focusing on government and public institutions.

## Examples

- **Athens, 5th century BCE**: Male citizens voted directly on laws in the *ekklesia* (assembly), one of the earliest documented democratic systems. Women, enslaved people, and non-citizens were excluded.
- **Iran, 1906–1911**: The Constitutional Revolution (انقلاب مشروطه) established Iran's first parliament (Majles) and constitution, shifting some power from the Qajar monarchy to elected representatives.
- **South Africa, 1994**: The first universal-suffrage election ended the apartheid system, in which political rights had been restricted by race since 1948.
