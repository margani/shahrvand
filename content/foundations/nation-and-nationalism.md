## Definition

A nation is a group of people who share a common identity, culture, historical memory, and often language, coupled with a shared aspiration for political self-determination. Nationalism is the political ideology and principle asserting that the nation should be the fundamental unit of human social life and that the political and national unit should be congruent — meaning each nation has the rightful claim to its own sovereign [state](the-state). Unlike ethnic identity, which is primarily cultural, national identity is inherently political, seeking institutional self-rule.

## Why it matters

Nationalism has been the most powerful political force reshaping the global map over the past three centuries. It dismantled dynastic empires (such as the Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman, and Russian empires), drove 20th-century anti-colonial liberation movements across Asia and Africa, and underpins the modern international system based on the sovereign nation-state. Understanding nationalism is essential for analyzing interstate conflicts, minority secessionist movements, border disputes, immigration debates, and citizenship laws.

## How it works

### Theories of the nation

Scholars dispute how and when nations emerged, dividing broadly into three schools of thought (Anderson, 1983; Gellner, 1983; Smith, 1986):

- **Modernism**: views nations as relatively recent constructs of modernity, industrialization, and mass education. Ernest Gellner argued that modern industrial economies required culturally homogeneous populations, compelling states to construct unified national identities (Gellner, 1983). Benedict Anderson famously defined the nation as an "imagined community" — imagined because members will never know most of their fellows, yet maintain a profound mental image of communion made possible by print-capitalism (Anderson, 1983).
- **Ethno-symbolism**: Anthony D. Smith argued that modern nations are built upon preexisting ethnic cores (*ethnies*) possessing shared myths of descent, historical memories, symbols, and values, rather than being invented out of thin air (Smith, 1986).
- **Primordialism**: an older view holding that nations are ancient, natural divisions of humanity rooted in biology, language, or ancestral soil. While largely discarded by contemporary social scientists, primordial thinking remains widespread in popular nationalist rhetoric.

### Civic vs. Ethnic nationalism

Political scientists commonly distinguish between two contrasting models of nationalism:

- **Civic (territorial) nationalism**: defines national membership by shared political values, citizenship, and allegiance to common constitutional principles, regardless of ancestry, ethnicity, or race (e.g., post-revolutionary France, the United States).
- **Ethnic (cultural) nationalism**: defines national membership by shared ancestry, native language, cultural inheritance, or bloodline, viewing citizenship as an inherited birthright rather than a political choice (e.g., 19th-century German Romantic nationalism).

## Contested points

- **Emancipatory vs. Exclusive**: defenders celebrate nationalism as an engine of democracy and collective self-determination, giving citizens a shared home and solidarity necessary for [welfare state](socialism-and-social-democracy) redistributive policies. Critics counter that nationalism is inherently exclusionary, prone to chauvinism, xenophobia, militarism, and the violent oppression of internal minorities.
- **National self-determination vs. Territorial integrity**: international law affirms both the right of peoples to self-determination (UN Charter, Article 1; ICCPR, Article 1) and the inviolability of state borders. This creates persistent legal and geopolitical conflicts when sub-state minorities (e.g., Kurds, Catalans, Scots) seek independent statehood against sovereign central governments.
- **Can nationalism survive globalization?** Globalists predicted that transnational economic integration, digital communication, and international governance (e.g., the European Union) would erode national attachments. Instead, recent decades have witnessed a sharp resurgence of nationalist movements reasserting sovereignty and border control.

## Examples

- **Anti-colonial revolutions, mid-20th century**: movements across India, Algeria, and Southeast Asia mobilized nationalist anti-imperialist solidarity to overthrow European colonial dominance and establish independent republics.
- **Breakup of Yugoslavia, 1990s**: the collapse of federal communist rule unleashed aggressive ethnic nationalism among Serbs, Croats, and Bosniaks, resulting in devastating wars, ethnic cleansing, and state fragmentation.
- **Iran**: Iranian national identity represents a complex synthesis of pre-Islamic heritage, Persian linguistic and literary traditions, Shia religious culture, and anti-colonial sovereignty. Modern Iranian nationalism was articulated during the 1906 Constitutional Revolution and reached peak popular mobilization under Mohammad Mossadegh during the 1951–1953 nationalization of oil, defending sovereign rights against foreign concessionary dominance.
