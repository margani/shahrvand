## Definition

Secularism is the political principle advocating the separation of religious institutions from the apparatus of the [state](the-state) and the independence of public law, education, and civil administration from religious authority. Coined in 1851 by George Jacob Holyoake, it describes a social order oriented toward human well-being in this life, without reference to theological doctrines. In modern constitutional democracies, secularism entails state neutrality toward diverse religions, equal treatment of believers and non-believers, and the protection of universal freedom of conscience.

## Why it matters

Managing religious diversity peacefully is a central challenge of governance. Without secular principles, states frequently privilege a dominant faith, turning religious minorities into second-class citizens through legal discrimination, blasphemy laws, or sectarian exclusion. A secular constitutional framework provides the legal space within which individuals of all faiths — or none — can coexist under equal [rule of law](../rights/rights-and-rule-of-law).

## How it works

### Core constitutional models

Comparative constitutional scholars distinguish several models of secular statecraft (Taylor, 2007; Bhargava, 1998):

- **Strict separation (The American model)**: codified in the First Amendment to the U.S. Constitution (1791), establishing a "wall of separation" between church and state. The government cannot establish an official religion, but individuals enjoy broad freedom to express their faith publicly.
- **Assertive secularism (*Laïcité* in France)**: emerging from the 1905 law on the Separation of Churches and the State. It keeps the public sphere (especially public schools and state offices) strictly free from visible religious symbols, treating religion as a private matter.
- **Principled distance (The Indian model)**: the state does not enforce mutual exclusion but maintains "principled distance," intervening neutrally to reform oppressive practices (e.g., banning untouchability) while assisting religious communities equally (Bhargava, 1998).

### Institutional pillars

A modern secular democracy generally rests on three commitments:
1. **Freedom of conscience**: the right to hold, change, or abandon any religious belief without legal penalty (UDHR, Article 18).
2. **Equality and non-discrimination**: civil rights, political office, and public employment are open to all citizens irrespective of faith.
3. **Statutory independence**: laws are enacted through elected legislatures rather than clerical decrees, and courts interpret civil statutes rather than sacred doctrine.

## Contested points

- **Neutrality vs. Anti-religious bias**: critics argue that assertive secularism (such as French *laïcité*) ceases to be neutral, restricting religious freedoms by prohibiting religious attire in public institutions. Defenders counter that visible neutrality is essential to protect citizens from sectarian pressure.
- **Is secularism culturally specific?** Post-colonial critics (Asad, 2003) argue that secularism reflects Western Christian history and should not be imposed universally. Defenders reply that the need to prevent state-sponsored religious oppression is universal.
- **Religious speech in democratic debate**: political theorists debate whether religious arguments should be permitted in lawmaking. While secular theorists argue coercive laws must be defended using shared public reason (Rawls, 1993), religious advocates maintain that excluding faith-based reasoning unfairly silences religious citizens.

## Examples

- **France, 1905**: the law on the Separation of the Churches and the State ended official Catholic establishment, declaring that the republic neither recognizes nor subsidizes any religion.
- **India, 1950**: the constitution crafted under B. R. Ambedkar established a secular republic in a multi-faith society, balancing religious freedom with state intervention to eliminate caste discrimination.
- **Iran**: modern Iranian history reflects continuous contestation over secularism. Reza Shah enforced secular modernization (curbing clerical courts and banning the veil in 1936). The 1979 Revolution replaced this with a theocratic republic (*velayat-e faqih*) where Islamic jurisprudence governs statutory law, provoking ongoing domestic debates over religious governance versus secular democracy.
