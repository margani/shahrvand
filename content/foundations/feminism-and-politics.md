## Definition

Feminism is a broad tradition of political theory and social movement aimed at understanding, critiquing, and transforming systems of gender-based inequality, discrimination, and subordination. Its central premise is that society has historically been organized along patriarchal lines — systems of social and political power in which men disproportionately hold primary authority, institutional leadership, and moral privilege. Feminism seeks the social, political, economic, and legal equality of all genders.

## Why it matters

Feminism has fundamentally reshaped modern political life, expanding the very definition of politics beyond parliaments and courts into the spheres of family life, bodily autonomy, and labor relations. Feminist activism secured foundational democratic rights: universal female suffrage, access to higher education and professions, reproductive freedoms, equal pay legislation, and legal protections against domestic violence and sexual assault. Understanding feminism is crucial for analyzing citizenship, human rights, labor economics, and social justice globally.

## How it works

### The historical "waves" of feminism

Historians commonly categorize the evolution of Western feminist thought into four overlapping waves (Freedman, 2002):

- **First Wave (late 19th to early 20th century)**: focused primarily on legal and political rights, notably women's suffrage (the right to vote), property rights for married women, and access to education (Mary Wollstonecraft, Emmeline Pankhurst).
- **Second Wave (1960s–1980s)**: addressed systemic cultural and economic inequalities, famously summarized in the slogan "the personal is political" (Carol Hanisch). It scrutinized workplace discrimination, reproductive rights, domestic violence, and unequal distribution of domestic labor (Simone de Beauvoir, Betty Friedan).
- **Third Wave (1990s–2000s)**: emphasized diversity, individual agency, and Kimberly Crenshaw's concept of *intersectionality* — the understanding that gender inequality intersects with race, class, sexuality, nationality, and disability to create interlocking systems of disadvantage (Crenshaw, 1989).
- **Fourth Wave (2010s to present)**: characterized by digital activism (such as the global #MeToo movement), focusing on combating sexual harassment, systemic rape culture, and expanding transgender rights.

### Major theoretical traditions

- **Liberal feminism**: seeks equal legal rights, non-discrimination, and representation within existing constitutional and market frameworks through legal reforms and policy changes.
- **Radical feminism**: views patriarchy as the most fundamental, universal form of social oppression, arguing that minor legal adjustments within male-dominated systems are insufficient and calling for revolutionary restructuring of gender roles and institutions.
- **Socialist and Marxist feminism**: argues that gender oppression is inextricably intertwined with capitalist exploitation, emphasizing that women's unpaid domestic labor underpins the market economy.

## Contested points

- **"The personal is political"**: critics argue that expanding the domain of politics to interpersonal relationships, marriage, and family life intrudes excessively into private life, potentially inviting state interference into intimate affairs. Feminists counter that treating the home as private and apolitical historically shielded domestic violence, marital rape, and economic exploitation from legal remedy.
- **Equality vs. Difference**: a long-standing debate within feminist theory divides thinkers who advocate gender blindness (treating men and women identically before the law) from those who argue that legal systems must account for physiological and structural differences (e.g., maternity leave, reproductive healthcare).
- **Universalism vs. Imperialism**: post-colonial and non-Western feminists critique mainstream Western feminism for portraying women in developing or Muslim-majority nations as passive victims in need of Western liberation, emphasizing that indigenous feminist struggles must define their own priorities rooted in local histories and religious contexts (Mohanty, 1988).

## Examples

- **Universal suffrage victories (1893–1928)**: New Zealand became the first self-governing country to grant women the right to vote in 1893, followed by the United Kingdom (1918/1928) and the United States (1920 with the 19th Amendment), fundamentally broadening democratic [legitimacy](legitimacy).
- **CEDAW (1979)**: the UN Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women, often described as an international bill of rights for women, ratified by 189 states.
- **Iran**: Iranian women have been central political actors since the 1906 Constitutional Revolution (forming secret societies and schools). Following the 1979 Revolution and the imposition of mandatory dress codes and legal restrictions, Iranian women led influential advocacy efforts — including the One Million Signatures Campaign (2006) and the massive "Woman, Life, Freedom" (*Zan, Zendegi, Azadi*) protests in 2022 following the death of Jina Mahsa Amini, placing gender equality at the forefront of Iranian political discourse.
