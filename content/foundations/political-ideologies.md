## Definition

A political ideology is a coherent system of ideas, values, and beliefs that provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how human society functions and offers a normative blueprint for how political [power](power-and-authority) should be organized. Ideologies explain the current social and political order, evaluate whether that order is just or flawed, and mobilize collective action to preserve or transform it. Key modern ideologies include liberalism, socialism, conservatism, nationalism, and various forms of religious politics.

## Why it matters

Ideologies shape how people interpret everyday news, formulate public policies, and choose among competing candidates in [elections](../institutions/elections-and-voting-systems). They provide the intellectual vocabulary behind laws, tax debates, welfare systems, and foreign policy alliances. Without an understanding of political ideologies, citizens often perceive political debates as chaotic clashes of personalities rather than structured disputes between fundamentally different views of human nature, freedom, equality, and justice.

## How it works

### Core components of an ideology

Political theorists identify three central dimensions in every political ideology (Heywood, 2021; Freeden, 1996):

- **A critique of the present**: an analysis explaining why certain social conditions (poverty, moral decline, inequality, tyranny) exist.
- **A vision of the ideal future**: an inspiring picture of a good society, whether organized around personal liberty, collective solidarity, tradition, or religious righteousness.
- **A theory of political action**: a strategy outlining how to get from the present to the desired future, whether through gradual legislative reform, civil disobedience, or revolutionary upheaval.

### The Left-Right political spectrum

The widespread division of politics into "left" and "right" originated during the French National Assembly of 1789, where members who supported the monarchy and traditional hierarchy sat on the right of the assembly presiding officer, while those who favored revolutionary change and egalitarian reform sat on the left.
- **The Left**: generally prioritizes social and economic equality, public services, collective welfare, and reform of established hierarchies.
- **The Right**: generally emphasizes individual enterprise, private property, the preservation of established traditions, and social order.
- **Multi-dimensional models**: contemporary political scientists emphasize that a simple one-dimensional line cannot capture modern politics; views on economic freedom (state intervention vs. free markets) often crosscut views on social or cultural issues (individual autonomy vs. traditional moral order).

## Contested points

- **Are ideologies inherently dangerous dogmas?** Thinkers like Karl Popper (1945) warned that rigid ideologies encourage dogmatism, intolerance of criticism, and totalitarian coercion by claiming to possess absolute historical truth. Defenders counter that everyone possesses an underlying ideological worldview whether conscious of it or not, and that articulate ideologies make political values transparent and debatable.
- **The "End of Ideology" thesis**: in the late 1950s and again after the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1989 (Fukuyama, 1992), scholars suggested that ideological conflict had ended with the universal triumph of liberal democratic capitalism. However, subsequent resurgences of populism, nationalism, socialist revival, and political Islam demonstrated that ideological contestation remains vigorous.
- **Ideology vs. pragmatism**: politicians frequently claim to be purely "pragmatic" problem-solvers rather than ideological. Political scientists note that definitions of what is "practical" or "common sense" always reflect implicit ideological assumptions about what outcomes are desirable.

## Examples

- **France, 1789**: the French Revolution served as the crucible of modern ideological politics, producing early formulations of liberalism (human rights and popular sovereignty), radical egalitarianism, and modern conservatism.
- **Cold War (1947–1991)**: global political competition between the United States and the Soviet Union was fought primarily along ideological lines, contrasting liberal capitalism with Marxist-Leninist state socialism.
- **Iran, 20th century**: modern Iranian history illustrates profound ideological contestation: constitutional liberalism during the 1906 Revolution, anti-imperialist nationalism and socialism (Mossadegh's National Front and the Tudeh Party), Pahlavi state-modernist nationalism, and the synthesis of anti-monarchical populism with Shia political theology (*velayat-e faqih*) in 1979.
