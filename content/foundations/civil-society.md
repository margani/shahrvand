## Definition

Civil society comprises the arena of voluntary collective action formed around shared interests, purposes, and values, distinct from both the formal apparatus of the [state](the-state) and the commercial marketplace. It encompasses an array of non-state organizations: non-governmental organizations (NGOs), trade unions, community initiatives, professional syndicates, religious charities, independent media, and grassroots advocacy campaigns. Civil society provides the civic space in which citizens organize autonomously to voice demands, deliver community services, and hold authorities accountable.

## Why it matters

A healthy civil society is widely regarded by political scientists as the social foundation of stable [legitimacy](legitimacy) and [the rule of law](../rights/rights-and-rule-of-law). While [constitutions](../institutions/constitutions) and elections provide formal institutional rules, it is civil society that breathes life into them: citizens monitoring election counts, defending civil liberties in court, demanding labour safety, and mobilizing aid in emergencies. Where civil society is weak, atomized, or suppressed, states tend to become unaccountable, leaving citizens with few tools other than silence or sudden upheaval.

## How it works

### Core functions of civic associations

Political sociologists identify four key contributions made by civil society (Putnam, 1993; Diamond, 1994):

- **Limiting and monitoring state power**: watching government agencies, uncovering public corruption, and protecting human rights against arbitrary enforcement.
- **Civic education and democratic socialization**: training participants in negotiation, deliberation, tolerance for differing views, and public leadership.
- **Interest representation**: aggregating concerns that [political parties](../institutions/political-parties) may overlook, such as minority rights, environmental conservation, and local workplace standards.
- **Building social capital**: cultivating interpersonal trust and norms of reciprocity that enable citizens to solve collective action problems without relying exclusively on state coercion (Putnam, 1993).

### The legal environment

Civil society requires a permissive legal environment to function effectively. Under international human rights law, this space is secured by fundamental freedoms:
- **Freedom of association**: protected by Article 22 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR, 1966).
- **Freedom of peaceful assembly**: protected by Article 21 of the ICCPR.
- **Freedom of expression and information**: protected by Article 19 of the ICCPR.

## Contested points

- **Does civil society inherently promote democracy?** While early democratic theory viewed civil associations as inherently virtuous, scholars note that civil society can also include anti-democratic groups, ethnic militias, and extremist movements. Robert Putnam (2000) differentiated between "bonding social capital" (which reinforces insular group loyalty) and "bridging social capital" (which connects diverse social groups).
- **The "NGO-ization" critique**: critics from both left and right argue that heavily funded non-governmental organizations can become detached from grassroots populations, acting as professionalized bureaucracies that manage social grievances without challenging deeper structural problems.
- **Foreign funding vs. sovereignty**: many governments regulate or ban foreign funding for domestic civic groups, arguing that foreign donations constitute external interference in national sovereignty. International human rights organizations counter that such restrictions are routinely weaponized by authoritarian regimes to starve independent watchdogs of resources.

## Examples

- **Poland, 1980–1989**: the independent trade union *Solidarność* (Solidarity), led by Lech Wałęsa, mobilized millions of workers and intellectuals outside state control, playing a pivotal role in the peaceful collapse of the communist regime.
- **United States, 1950s–1960s**: the American Civil Rights Movement, anchored in Black churches, student groups, and community organizations, dismantled legal segregation through non-violent boycotts, marches, and strategic legal challenges.
- **Iran**: civic organization has a long and resilient history, from traditional guilds and bazaar networks during the 1906 Constitutional Revolution to professional associations (the Iranian Bar Association founded in 1931), women's rights campaigns (such as the One Million Signatures Campaign, 2006), and charitable foundations. Iranian civil organizations frequently navigate state licensing requirements and national security scrutiny.
