## Definition

Fascism is an extreme right-wing, ultranationalist political ideology and mass movement that emerged in early 20th-century Europe. It is characterized by dictatorial executive power, aggressive militarism, the subordination of individual interests to the perceived needs of the national or racial collective, and the violent suppression of democratic opposition. The historian Roger Griffin formulated its influential concise definition as a form of "palingenetic ultranationalism" — a mythic ideology seeking the revolutionary rebirth (*palingenesis*) of a nation deemed to be decaying under liberalism, decadence, and class struggle (Griffin, 1991).

## Why it matters

Fascism represents the most destructive political phenomenon of the 20th century, culminating in World War II and the Holocaust. Understanding the ideological mechanisms and historical preconditions of fascism — economic devastation, social humiliation, scapegoating of vulnerable minorities, erosion of constitutional norms, and the glorification of violence — provides essential analytical tools for recognizing and guarding against radical anti-democratic movements in contemporary politics.

## How it works

### Core structural traits of fascism

Scholars identify several interrelated pillars common to historical fascist regimes (Paxton, 2004; Eco, 1995):

- **Ultranationalism and rebirth**: portraying the nation as an organic community possessing a sacred destiny, threatened by internal traitors and external enemies, requiring total national regeneration.
- **The cult of the leader (Führerprinzip)**: blind obedience to an infallible, charismatic supreme leader who claims directly to embody the national will and commands absolute authority above the law.
- **Militarism and glorification of violence**: viewing struggle and war as natural tests of vitality that purify and strengthen the national character, subordinating civil society to martial discipline and paramilitary organizations (e.g., the Italian Blackshirts, the German Stormtroopers).
- **Anti-liberalism and anti-Marxism**: fierce hostility toward parliamentary democracy, individual civil liberties, and the socialist concept of class struggle, arguing that both liberal individualism and socialist division tear the national collective apart.
- **Corporatist economic organization**: organizing economic life into state-managed employer and worker syndicates, leaving private property and corporate profits intact while stripping independent trade unions of the right to strike and subordinating business to state military aims.

### Paxton's anatomy of fascism

Historian Robert Paxton emphasized that fascism is best understood not merely as static doctrine, but as a dynamic political process moving through stages: from intellectual alienation and mass recruitment, to entering electoral alliances with traditional conservative elites, seizing power, and radicalizing into war and mass violence (Paxton, 2004).

## Contested points

- **Generic fascism vs. Nazi uniqueness**: scholars debate whether Italian Fascism (Mussolini) and German National Socialism (Hitler) belong to the same ideological genus. While Italian Fascism emphasized total state supremacy ("Everything in the State, nothing outside the State, nothing against the State"), German Nazism subordinated even the state apparatus to biological, pseudoscientific racial purity and anti-Semitism. Most comparative political scientists treat Nazism as a radical, racialized variant of the broader fascist family.
- **Fascism as an insult vs. analytical category**: in contemporary public discourse, the label "fascist" is frequently used as a loose political insult to discredit conservative or authoritarian opponents. Political scientists insist on rigorous definitions, warning that careless usage trivializes historical atrocities and obscures the distinct traits of actual neo-fascist movements.
- **Capitalism and fascism**: Marxist theorists historically characterized fascism as "the open terrorist dictatorship of the most reactionary elements of finance capital." Liberal and conservative historians counter that fascist regimes aggressively subordinated corporate capital to ideological warfare and state commands, demonstrating that fascism was a revolutionary ideological force in its own right.

## Examples

- **Italy under Benito Mussolini (1922–1943)**: the birthplace of modern fascism; following the 1922 March on Rome, Mussolini dismantled parliamentary democracy, banned opposition parties, established a corporatist state, and launched imperialist conquests in Ethiopia.
- **Nazi Germany under Adolf Hitler (1933–1945)**: the most extreme and catastrophic manifestation of fascism, combining state terror, industrial rearmament, territorial expansionism, and the systematic industrial genocide of six million European Jews and millions of others.
- **Spain under Francisco Franco (1939–1975)**: while incorporating the fascist *Falange* movement during the Spanish Civil War, Franco's regime transitioned into a traditional military and Catholic authoritarian dictatorship, maintaining neutrality in World War II.
