title: Diplomacy
description: The methods, institutions, and communication channels through which sovereign states negotiate and manage international relations.

## Definition
Diplomacy is the established practice, art, and institutionalized communication through which sovereign states and international actors conduct formal relations, negotiate treaties, resolve disputes, and advance strategic interests without resorting to armed conflict. Rather than relying on military violence or unilateral coercion, diplomacy uses dialogue, compromise, protocol, and multilateral consensus.

Codified universally under the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (1961), modern diplomacy encompasses permanent resident embassies, diplomatic immunity, bilateral negotiations, multilateral summits, and public diplomacy aimed at foreign populations.

## Why it matters
Diplomacy is the fundamental mechanism that prevents friction in the anarchic international system from escalating into catastrophic warfare. It provides institutional communication channels between adversarial states even during heightened crises, allowing governments to clarify intentions, defuse miscalculations, and negotiate armistices.

Beyond security, modern diplomacy facilitates the complex cross-border cooperation that sustains global civilization. International agreements governing global civil aviation, climate emissions, telecommunications protocols, international trade rules, and pandemic containment are all products of continuous multilateral diplomatic negotiations.

## How it works
Diplomatic engagement functions through diverse formal and informal formats:

1. **Bilateral and Multilateral Diplomacy**: Bilateral diplomacy involves direct, state-to-state relations conducted through accredited ambassadors and embassies. Multilateral diplomacy occurs within institutional bodies (such as the UN, WTO, or regional blocs) where multiple nations negotiate common conventions.
2. **Diplomatic Immunity and Protocol**: Under the Vienna Convention of 1961, diplomatic personnel and premises enjoy legal inviolability, exempting diplomats from host-country prosecution and ensuring confidential communications between ambassadors and their home capitals.
3. **Summitry and Shuttle Diplomacy**: Direct face-to-face negotiations between heads of state or high-level mediators travelling rapidly between hostile capitals to forge compromises.
4. **Track I and Track II Diplomacy**: Track I refers to official, formal negotiations between state officials. Track II involves informal, backchannel dialogues between non-governmental experts, academics, or retired officials designed to build trust when official channels are politically blocked.
5. **Coercive Diplomacy and "Gunboat" Tactics**: Combining diplomatic negotiations with explicit, credible threats of military action or economic sanctions to compel an adversary to capitulate without firing a shot.
6. **Public Diplomacy**: Direct communication by a government to foreign publics through international broadcasting, cultural exchanges, and educational scholarships to foster favourable perceptions.

## Contested points
The role, transparency, and morality of diplomatic statecraft are subject to ongoing controversy.

A historic debate contrasts open diplomacy with secret negotiations. In his Fourteen Points (1918), Woodrow Wilson championed "open covenants of peace, openly arrived at," arguing that secret treaties and clandestine alliances had dragged Europe into the horrors of World War I. However, realist practitioners argue that total public transparency makes compromise politically impossible; politicians cannot make difficult concessions under the immediate gaze of partisan domestic media without being accused of treason, making confidential backchannels essential for breakthroughs.

Another dispute involves the moral ethics of negotiating with brutal dictatorships. Critics argue that engaging diplomatically with autocratic regimes that commit gross human rights abuses grants them undeserved international legitimacy and betrays oppressed populations. Conversely, pragmatic diplomats argue that diplomacy is not an endorsement of virtue, but a practical tool to manage conflict, asserting that one does not make peace with friends, but with dangerous adversaries.

## Examples
The Congress of Vienna (1814–1815) established the "Concert of Europe" following the Napoleonic Wars. Orchestrated by Austrian diplomat Klemens von Metternich and British foreign secretary Lord Castlereagh, the congress restored a balance of power through negotiated compromise, securing general peace among European great powers for nearly a century.

In the 20th century, the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis demonstrated crisis diplomacy at its most critical. US President John F. Kennedy and Soviet Premier Nikita Khrushchev utilized secret backchannels—supplemented by an informal agreement to remove US missiles from Turkey in exchange for the withdrawal of Soviet missiles from Cuba—averting a nuclear catastrophe.

In Iranian modern history, the 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA) stands as a landmark of intensive multilateral diplomacy. Negotiated over nearly two years by Iran and the P5+1 (the US, UK, France, Russia, China, and Germany), the accord demonstrated that even deeply entrenched ideological adversaries could achieve a comprehensive technical agreement, exchanging verifiable caps on uranium enrichment for sanctions relief. However, the unilateral withdrawal of the US in 2018 highlighted the vulnerability of diplomatic agreements that lack formal domestic treaty ratification.
