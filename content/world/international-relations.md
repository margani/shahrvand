## Definition
International relations is the academic discipline and political practice concerning the interactions between sovereign states, intergovernmental bodies, multinational corporations, and non-state actors across national borders. Because there is no overarching world government with coercive authority to enforce global laws, the international system exists in a condition of structural anarchy.

Political scientists analyze international relations through competing theoretical paradigms—most prominently Realism, Liberalism, and Constructivism—each offering divergent explanations of why states cooperate, compete, balance power, or go to war.

## Why it matters
The dynamics of international relations dictate the most existential questions of human civilization: the prevention of nuclear war, the regulation of global trade, collective action on climate change, and the protection of international human rights. A breakdown in international stability can trigger geopolitical conflict, economic collapse, and mass refugee crises across continents.

For individual citizens, foreign policy decisions determine whether their nations enter armed conflicts, how resources are allocated between domestic welfare and military defense, and whether cross-border movement, commerce, and communication remain open.

## How it works
The international system functions through several structural principles and institutions:

1. **State Sovereignty and Anarchy**: Originating in the 1648 Peace of Westphalia, state sovereignty asserts that each state possesses exclusive political authority within its borders. Anarchy means that in the absence of a global police force, states must ultimately rely on "self-help."
2. **Balance of Power and Polarity**: States manage security through alliances and military deterrence. The international system can be unipolar (dominated by one superpower, as in the post-Cold War US era), bipolar (the US-Soviet Cold War), or multipolar (multiple competing powers, as in 19th-century Europe and increasingly today).
3. **The Security Dilemma**: Coined by John Herz, this concept describes how actions taken by one state to increase its own security (such as building up arms) inadvertently threaten neighbours, triggering reciprocal military expansion and heightening the risk of unintended conflict.
4. **International Law and Regimes**: Treaties, customary international law, and multilateral forums (such as the United Nations and the International Court of Justice) provide rules and norms that facilitate cooperation and reduce transaction costs.
5. **Deterrence and Coercion**: States project influence through "hard power" (military and economic coercion) and "soft power" (cultural attraction, diplomatic values, and institutional legitimacy).

## Contested points
The governing logic of international politics is contested among three foundational paradigms:

Realists (such as Hans Morgenthau and Kenneth Waltz) argue that international politics is an unending, zero-sum struggle for survival and power under anarchy. In their view, moral values and international institutions are largely illusions; states act exclusively out of national self-interest, and peace is maintained only through a robust balance of military power and deterrence.

Liberals and Neoliberal Institutionalists (such as Robert Keohane and Joseph Nye) contest this pessimism. They argue that economic interdependence, democratic governance ("democratic peace theory"), and international institutions enable durable cooperation. By creating shared rules, monitoring compliance, and fostering trade, international institutions can mitigate anarchy and generate positive-sum collective outcomes.

Constructivists (such as Alexander Wendt) argue that the international system is not an objective material structure, but a social construct. As Wendt famously stated, "anarchy is what states make of it." They argue that state interests, alliances, and enmities are defined by identities, ideas, and shared historical norms rather than raw military balances alone.

## Examples
The Cold War (1947–1991) exemplified a bipolar balance of power. The ideological, geopolitical, and military rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union manifested in proxy conflicts (Korea, Vietnam, Angola) and an unprecedented nuclear arms race. The doctrine of Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD) established a perilous nuclear deterrence balance preventing direct superpower war.

The post-Cold War expansion of the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) and the European Union reflected liberal institutionalist faith in democratic expansion. However, Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine marked a resurgence of realist power politics, territorial aggression, and great-power proxy conflict in Europe.

Iran's international relations reflect a tense blend of revolutionary ideology and pragmatic geopolitical balancing. Following the 1979 Revolution, Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini articulated the doctrine "Neither East nor West" (نه شرقی، نه غربی), challenging both superpowers and calling for Islamic unity. Over subsequent decades, faced with regional containment by the US and Gulf monarchies, Iran cultivated the "Axis of Resistance" (non-state allied militias in Lebanon, Iraq, Syria, and Yemen) as a forward-defense strategy, while pragmatically deepening strategic and economic partnerships with China and Russia to counter Western isolation.
