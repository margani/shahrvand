## Definition
The United Nations (UN) is an intergovernmental organisation founded in 1945 following the devastation of World War II, dedicated to maintaining international peace and security, developing friendly relations among nations, fostering international economic and social cooperation, and harmonizing the actions of states. Headquartered in New York City, it is the premier multilateral body in world politics, comprising 193 member states.

The UN operates on the basis of its founding Charter, which enshrines the sovereign equality of all member states, prohibits the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state (Article 2(4)), and obligates members to settle disputes peacefully.

## Why it matters
The UN is the foundational pillar of the modern multilateral rules-based international order. It provides an indispensable diplomatic arena where every recognised sovereign nation possesses a voice, ensuring continuous communication even between warring adversaries.

Through specialized agencies like WHO, UNHCR, and WFP, the UN coordinates global humanitarian relief, poverty alleviation, and pandemic response, while codifying international legal norms on human rights and armed conflict.

## How it works
The United Nations functions through six principal organs established by its Charter:

1. **The General Assembly (UNGA)**: The main deliberative body, where all 193 member states have equal representation (one state, one vote). UNGA resolutions on peace and security are non-binding recommendations, but carry substantial moral and political weight.
2. **The Security Council (UNSC)**: Charged with primary responsibility for maintaining international peace and security. It consists of 15 members: five permanent members (P5—the US, UK, France, Russia, and China) holding veto power, and ten non-permanent members elected for two-year terms. Under Chapter VII of the Charter, UNSC resolutions are legally binding on all members and can authorize economic sanctions, arms embargoes, or military force.
3. **The Secretariat**: Headed by the Secretary-General, it carries out the administrative and operational work of the organization, directing peacekeeping missions and mediation efforts.
4. **The International Court of Justice (ICJ)**: Based in The Hague, it adjudicates legal disputes submitted by states and issues advisory opinions on international legal questions.
5. **The Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC)**: Coordinates economic, social, environmental, and humanitarian fields, overseeing specialized functional commissions.
6. **Peacekeeping Operations**: Deployed with the consent of host nations to monitor ceasefires, protect civilians, and assist post-conflict reconstruction, traditionally guided by principles of consent, impartiality, and non-use of force except in self-defence.

## Contested points
The structural legitimacy and operational effectiveness of the United Nations are subjects of intense global contestation.

The most prominent criticism centres on the composition and veto power of the Security Council's permanent five members (P5). Critics argue that the P5 structure reflects the geopolitical realities of 1945, not the 21st century. Major emerging powers (such as India, Brazil, South Africa, and Germany) remain excluded from permanent seats. Furthermore, the veto power routinely paralyzes the Council during major crises; when a permanent member or its close ally is directly involved in aggression or human rights abuses (such as Russia in Ukraine, or the US concerning Israel and Palestine), the Council is blocked from taking effective collective action.

Conversely, defenders of the veto argue that it was precisely the veto power that kept the nuclear-armed superpowers within the UN framework throughout the Cold War, preventing the organization from collapsing as the League of Nations did in the 1930s.

Another controversy involves the "Responsibility to Protect" (R2P) doctrine adopted in 2005. Proponents argue that states forfeit sovereignty if they commit atrocities against their own people, requiring international intervention; critics contend R2P is selectively weaponised by great powers for regime change.

## Examples
The Korean War (1950–1953) marked the UN's first collective military enforcement action. Taking advantage of a Soviet boycott of the Security Council, the Council passed Resolution 83 authorizing an international military coalition under US command to repel North Korea's invasion of South Korea.

In contrast, the catastrophic failures of UN peacekeeping in the 1990s—most tragically during the 1994 Rwandan Genocide and the 1995 Srebrenica Massacre in Bosnia—highlighted the limits of lightly armed peacekeepers operating under weak, ambiguous mandates without political support from great powers.

Iran was an original founding member in 1945. Following the 1979 Revolution, relations grew fraught. During the 1980–1988 Iran-Iraq War, Iran criticized Council inaction until Resolution 598. Between 2006 and 2010, the Security Council enacted sanctions over its nuclear program. Furthermore, the UN Human Rights Council continuously monitors Iran, establishing a Fact-Finding Mission following the 2022 protests.
