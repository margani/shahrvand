## Definition

Corruption is broadly defined by political scientists and international legal bodies as the abuse of entrusted public [power](../foundations/power-and-authority) or public office for private or partisan gain (Transparency International; Rose-Ackerman, 1999). It encompasses bribery, embezzlement, nepotism, extortion, and state capture. Accountability is the fundamental constitutional principle and institutional mechanism requiring public officials — elected and appointed — to report, justify, and face legal or political consequences for their decisions, expenditure, and conduct (Schedler, 1999).

## Why it matters

Corruption is corrosive to democratic [legitimacy](../foundations/legitimacy) and economic development. When corruption becomes systemic, the [rule of law](../rights/rights-and-rule-of-law) is dismantled: public funds intended for clinics, schools, and roads are stolen; court judgments are purchased by the wealthy; and public faith in democratic institutions collapses, fueling cynicism and autocratic revanchism. Accountability mechanisms provide the institutional antidote to corruption, ensuring that authority is constrained by law and that power-holders remain servants of the public rather than self-serving oligarchs.

## How it works

### Forms of political corruption

Political economists categorize corruption into distinct levels (Klitgaard, 1988; Johnston, 2005):

- **Petty (administrative) corruption**: small-scale bribery paid by citizens to low-level bureaucrats to secure routine public services, permits, or evade police fines.
- **Grand (political) corruption**: high-level theft of state assets, kickbacks on major public procurement contracts, and illicit enrichment by senior ministers and heads of state.
- **State capture**: a systemic form of grand corruption where powerful private oligarchs, corporations, or military factions capture the legislative and regulatory machinery of the state, shaping laws and policies to advance their private profits.

### Dimensions of institutional accountability

Political theorist Andreas Schedler identified two complementary pillars of accountability (Schedler, 1999):
1. **Answerability**: the legal obligation of power-holders to provide transparent public information and justify their decisions.
2. **Enforcement**: the capacity of institutions to reward good governance and punish misconduct through legal sanctions, removal from office, or criminal prosecution.

These pillars operate through two axes:
- **Vertical accountability**: citizens holding rulers accountable directly from below through free and fair [elections](elections-and-voting-systems), investigative journalism, civil society mobilization, and recall procedures.
- **Horizontal accountability**: independent state oversight institutions checking power across branches from within: supreme audit courts, anti-corruption ombudsmen, ethics commissions, and independent [judiciaries](judiciary-and-courts).

## Contested points

- **Cultural relativism vs. Universal standards**: some critics argue that what Western observers label "nepotism" or "corruption" in developing nations represents traditional cultural obligations to assist family and kinship networks. The United Nations Convention Against Corruption (UNCAC, 2003) decisively rejected this, codifying universal definitions ratified by 190 states, emphasizing that bribery and embezzlement harm citizens regardless of cultural tradition.
- **Root cause: State regulation vs. Unregulated markets**: free-market economists argue that excessive state licensing, subsidies, and bureaucratic red tape create artificial monopolies that breed bribery (Robert Klitgaard's formula: *Corruption = Monopoly + Discretion - Accountability*). Conversely, critical political economists argue that unregulated privatization and political campaign spending enable corporate capture of public governance.
- **Anti-corruption campaigns as political weapons**: in authoritarian and competitive authoritarian regimes, anti-corruption crackdowns are frequently weaponized by ruling elites to purge political rivals, silence dissidents, and consolidate personal power rather than establishing genuine rule of law.

## Examples

- **Singapore**: transformed from a deeply corrupt colonial port in the 1950s into one of the world's least corrupt countries through the independent Corrupt Practices Investigation Bureau (CPIB), competitive market-level salaries for civil servants, and uncompromising enforcement across all political ranks.
- **Brazil (*Operação Lava Jato* / Operation Car Wash, 2014)**: a massive judicial investigation uncovering billions of dollars in systemic kickbacks between state oil company Petrobras, major construction conglomerates, and top politicians, demonstrating both the power and political vulnerabilities of independent judicial crusades.
- **Iran**: anti-corruption is a prominent official theme, yet institutional corruption remains widespread, driven by dual state structures, lack of media transparency, and opaque economic holdings controlled by foundations (*bonyads*) and military affiliates. High-profile embezzlement cases (such as the 2011 $2.6 billion banking scandal and petrochemical graft trials) regularly lead to show trials and executions, while critics argue that without free press oversight and independent civil society, systemic corruption cannot be uprooted.
