## Definition

Local government refers to the administrative, regulatory, and policy-making authorities established at the subnational municipal, county, or district level, representing the tier of public governance closest to everyday citizens. Decentralisation is the systematic transfer of decision-making authority, administrative responsibilities, and financial resources from central government ministries down to these lower-tier elected or administrative bodies (Rodden, 2004; Stoker, 1998).

## Why it matters

While national politics dominates news headlines, local governments make decisions that most directly affect daily human life: urban planning, zoning laws, water sanitation, public transport, waste disposal, local policing, and emergency services. Strong local government serves as an indispensable training ground for democratic citizenship, allowing ordinary people to deliberate, organize, and directly participate in managing their neighborhoods. Decentralization brings decision-making closer to local needs, preventing remote central bureaucrats from imposing uniform solutions on diverse communities.

## How it works

### Three dimensions of decentralisation

Political scientists and public administration scholars differentiate three distinct forms of decentralization (Schneider, 2003):

- **Political (democratic) decentralisation**: transferring decision-making power to locally elected councils and mayors, making local officials directly accountable to local voters rather than central ministries.
- **Administrative decentralisation (deconcentration)**: redistributing managerial responsibilities and personnel from capital-city headquarters to field offices in regional districts, while keeping ultimate policy control centralized.
- **Fiscal decentralisation**: granting local governments independent revenue-generating powers (local property taxes, business levies, utility fees) alongside predictable intergovernmental transfers from the national treasury.

### Common local government models

- **Mayor-Council system**: features an independently elected executive mayor and a separately elected legislative municipal council, mirroring the separation of powers (common in the United States and Latin America).
- **Council-Manager system**: an elected city council sets broad policy and appoints a professionally trained, non-partisan city manager to direct daily municipal operations.
- **Committee system**: all executive and administrative functions are managed directly by committees of elected councilors (common in traditional British local authorities).

## Contested points

- **Proximity vs. Local capture**: proponents argue local government ensures responsive, customized policies because local officials understand community needs better than central technocrats. Critics counter that local governments are especially vulnerable to "elite capture" — where wealthy landowners, local oligarchs, or mafia networks capture municipal councils and siphon off public funds far from national media scrutiny (Bardhan and Mookherjee, 2006).
- **Autonomy vs. Regional inequality**: giving local governments the power to fund schools and services from local tax bases causes severe geographic disparities, where wealthy suburbs enjoy pristine infrastructure while impoverished districts suffer underfunded clinics and crumbling schools. Centralized funding guarantees equal minimum standards across all regions.
- **Subsidiarity**: the principle of *subsidiarity* holds that social and political issues should be dealt with at the most immediate or local level that is consistent with their resolution. Debates persist over where local responsibility ends and national regulation (such as environmental standards and pandemic responses) must take precedence.

## Examples

- **Switzerland**: the most decentralized democracy in the world; its 2,100+ communes (*Gemeinden*) possess extensive fiscal autonomy, collect local income taxes, grant Swiss citizenship, and hold regular direct-democracy town meetings and referendums.
- **United States**: over 19,000 municipalities and 3,000 counties manage zoning, school boards, and municipal police forces, resulting in distinct local ordinances and tax rates across city boundaries.
- **Iran**: local governance is constitutionally structured around Islamic City and Village Councils (*Showra-ye Eslami-e Shahr va Roosta*), provided for under Chapter 7 of the Constitution (Articles 100–106). First formally implemented in nationwide elections in 1999 under President Mohammad Khatami, over 100,000 local councilors are elected every four years. While they select mayors and oversee municipal budgets, their authority is strictly administrative, subordinate to provincial governors (*ostandaran*) appointed by the central Ministry of Interior.
