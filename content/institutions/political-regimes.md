## Definition

A political system (or regime type) encompasses the formal and informal institutional rules that determine how political [power](../foundations/power-and-authority) is accessed, exercised, and limited within a [state](../foundations/the-state). Regime types are typically differentiated by who holds decision-making power, the degree of political competition permitted, and the scope of individual [rights and freedoms](../rights/rights-and-rule-of-law). The standard modern classification divides systems along a spectrum ranging from constitutional democracies to authoritarian and totalitarian regimes.

## Why it matters

The type of political regime under which individuals live determines almost every aspect of their civic and private lives: whether they can criticize leaders without fear of arrest, whether laws are publicly accountable, how securely property and contracts are protected, and whether government power is restrained. Comparing regime types provides the foundational vocabulary for analyzing international relations, democratization, and political decline.

## How it works

### The primary regime categories

Political scientists broadly classify political systems into three major categories, alongside hybrid models (Linz, 2000; Dahl, 1971):

- **Democratic regimes**: systems where rulers are chosen through competitive, free, and fair [elections](elections-and-voting-systems) with universal adult suffrage. Subdivided into:
  - *Liberal democracies*: institutionalize not only elections, but also strong constitutional limits on executive power, independent judiciaries, and comprehensive protection of civil liberties (Dahl's *polyarchy*).
  - *Electoral (illiberal) democracies*: conduct genuine electoral competition, but exhibit weak protections for civil liberties and rule-of-law norms (Zakaria, 1997).
- **Authoritarian regimes**: systems characterized by limited political pluralism, the suppression of meaningful opposition, and leadership that exercises power with minimal institutional accountability. Unlike totalitarians, authoritarian rulers often tolerate limited economic or religious autonomy provided it does not challenge the regime's political monopoly (Linz, 2000).
- **Totalitarian regimes**: an extreme form of non-democratic rule characterized by an all-encompassing official ideology, total state control over the economy, communication, and culture, and pervasive terror or surveillance through secret police and a single monopoly party (Arendt, 1951; Friedrich and Brzezinski, 1956).

### Hybrid regimes and competitive authoritarianism

Between pure democracy and closed dictatorship lies the widespread category of hybrid regimes (or "competitive authoritarianism"). In these systems, formal democratic institutions (elections, parliaments, courts) exist and form the primary arena for obtaining power, but incumbents systematically tilt the playing field using state media, patronage, and targeted harassment of opposition figures (Levitsky and Way, 2010).

## Contested points

- **Democracy as elections vs. substantive rights**: proceduralists define democracy strictly by the presence of competitive elections and citizen participation. Substantive theorists argue that a regime cannot be democratic without socioeconomic equality, judicial independence, and minority protections.
- **The "Developmental Autocracy" debate**: proponents of authoritarian models in rapid-growth states argue that centralizing power enables long-term infrastructure planning and social stability, citing rapid economic development in post-1960 Singapore or post-1978 China. Critics counter that without democratic accountability, autocratic systems generate systemic corruption, lack mechanisms for peaceful correction, and impose unacceptable human rights violations.
- **Democratic backsliding**: contemporary scholars debate why established democracies experience democratic decay. Some emphasize economic inequality and cultural backlash; others point to institutional decay where populist leaders weaken judicial checks and electoral bodies from within (Levitsky and Ziblatt, 2018).

## Examples

- **Germany and South Korea**: examples of consolidated liberal democracies featuring robust [separation of powers](separation-of-powers), active party competition, and enforceable constitutional protections.
- **Soviet Union under Stalin (1928–1953) and Nazi Germany (1933–1945)**: standard historical examples of totalitarian regimes where the state sought total control over civil society and private life through state ideology and terror.
- **Iran**: categorized by scholars as a complex hybrid regime combining theocratic oversight with competitive republican elements. While the presidency and parliament (Majles) are contested through popular elections, all candidates and laws are screened by unelected clerical bodies (the Guardian Council), and the Supreme Leader exercises ultimate constitutional command over the judiciary, military, and state media.
