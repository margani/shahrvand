## Definition

A political party is an organised group of people who share broadly similar political principles, policy goals, or group interests, and who seek to gain or retain governmental power, primarily through [elections](elections-and-voting-systems). Unlike single-issue lobby groups or interest groups, parties seek formal public office and address a wide spectrum of public policy. They serve as the primary bridge linking individual citizens and civil society to state institutions.

## Why it matters

Parties provide the structure through which modern representative government operates. They translate diverse public desires into coherent legislative agendas, present voters with clear policy alternatives, and groom future leaders. Where functioning parties are absent or suppressed, politics frequently becomes driven by opaque factions, personal patronage, or military force, making institutional accountability significantly harder for citizens to enforce.

## How it works

### Core functions of political parties

Political scientists identify several central functions performed by parties:

- **Candidate recruitment and leadership selection**: identifying, training, and running individuals for legislative and executive posts.
- **Interest aggregation**: gathering varied demands from disparate groups — workers, business owners, farmers, regional communities — into coherent political platforms.
- **Voter mobilization**: organizing campaigns, rallies, and civic education to encourage citizen turnout on election day.
- **Formulating policy**: debating policy priorities, drafting legislative programs, and presenting governing manifestos.
- **Organizing government and opposition**: in parliamentary systems, the majority party forms the government while minority parties form a loyal opposition that scrutinizes executive action (see [Separation of powers](separation-of-powers)).

### Internal organization and finance

Parties require formal structures to function: national committees, local branches, internal leadership elections, and conventions. Funding models vary substantially:
- **Public financing**: states provide subsidies based on past electoral results to reduce corruption and dependence on wealthy donors (e.g., Germany, France).
- **Private financing**: reliance on membership dues, private individual contributions, or corporate donations, which often raises questions about undue influence in politics.

## Contested points

- **Do parties divide or unite society?** Early constitutional thinkers, such as George Washington in his 1796 Farewell Address, viewed parties (or "factions") as divisive mechanisms that sow discord and undermine national unity. In contrast, modern political theorists argue that partisan organization is inevitable in a free society, providing peaceful, institutional channels to negotiate conflict.
- **The decline of party loyalty**: in many established democracies, party membership and voter identification with traditional parties have fallen over recent decades (Dalton and Wattenberg, 2002). Some see this as an erosion of democratic cohesion, while others view it as a sign of more independent, critical citizens.
- **State regulation vs. freedom of association**: international law, including Article 22 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR, 1966), protects the freedom of association. While states often regulate campaign spending or party registration, critics argue that excessive registration hurdles or party bans are frequently abused by incumbents to eliminate opposition.

## Examples

- **United Kingdom**: the Conservative Party (origins in the 17th-century Tories) and the Labour Party (founded in 1900 by trade unions and socialists) represent the traditional evolution of parliamentary parties structuring executive governance.
- **Iran, 1941–1953**: following the abdication of Reza Shah, Iran experienced an active period of competitive party politics, featuring parties such as the Tudeh Party (left-wing), the Iran Party, and the National Front coalition led by Mohammad Mossadegh, until the 1953 coup d'état curtailed open party activity.
- **Iran, post-1979**: the Islamic Republic initially saw party competition (such as the Islamic Republican Party, 1979–1987), but subsequently shifted to informal, fluid political factions (Principlists and Reformists) rather than durable, institutionalized political parties.
