## Definition

A constitution is the fundamental set of rules that defines how a [state](../foundations/the-state) is organised, how political [power](../foundations/power-and-authority) is distributed, and what limits are placed on government. It is the law that governs the making of all other laws. Most constitutions establish the structure of government, guarantee fundamental [rights](../rights/rights-and-rule-of-law), and set the rules for changing the constitution itself.

## Why it matters

Constitutions establish the rules of the political game. They specify who can hold public office, what the government may and may not do, and how disputes are resolved. Without a constitutional framework, political life is governed by raw power rather than predictable rules. Even authoritarian states usually have constitutions, though their willingness to respect those rules in practice varies widely.

## How it works

### Written and unwritten constitutions

Most states have a single written document, an arrangement widespread since the United States Constitution was adopted in 1788. A few states — notably the United Kingdom, New Zealand, and Israel — have unwritten constitutions, relying instead on a web of statutes, judicial rulings, and conventions.

### Core components

- **Structure of government**: defines the branches of power and their interactions. (See [Separation of powers](separation-of-powers).)
- **Bill of rights**: guarantees individual freedoms against state interference. (See [Rights and the rule of law](../rights/rights-and-rule-of-law).)
- **Amendment procedures**: specifies how constitutional rules can be altered, often requiring legislative supermajorities or popular referendums.
- **Supremacy**: establishes that constitutional rules override ordinary legislation.

### Constitutional review

Many systems allow courts to assess whether laws comply with the constitution. This practice, known as judicial review, was notably established in the United States in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) and is widely used today.

## Contested points

- **Democracy vs. constitutional constraint**: constitutions restrict majorities by design. Critics argue that rigid rules prevent contemporary electorates from addressing modern problems. Defenders respond that constraints protect individuals and minorities from majoritarian abuse (Tocqueville, 1835).
- **Words vs. political reality**: critics observe that constitutions often reflect existing power balances rather than constraining them. Whether a constitution restrains rulers depends on political culture and institutional enforcement, not the text alone.
- **The counter-majoritarian tension**: granting unelected judges the power to strike down laws passed by elected legislators creates friction between popular sovereignty and constitutional oversight (Bickel, 1962).

## Examples

- **United States, 1788**: created a federal republic with [separated powers](separation-of-powers), an explicit Bill of Rights (1791), and judicial review.
- **Iran, 1906 and 1979**: the 1906 Constitutional Revolution established Iran's first parliament (Majles) and constitutional limits on the Qajar monarchy. The 1979 constitution replaced this with the Islamic Republic, creating elected bodies (president, parliament) overseen by the Supreme Leader and the Guardian Council.
- **South Africa, 1996**: the post-apartheid constitution established a Constitutional Court and enshrined both civil-political liberties and socioeconomic rights (housing, healthcare).
- **Germany, 1949**: the Basic Law (*Grundgesetz*) includes an "eternity clause" (Article 79(3)) preventing amendments that alter basic human dignity, the democratic order, or federalism.
