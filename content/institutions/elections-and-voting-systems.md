## Definition

An election is a formal procedure through which citizens choose individuals to hold public office. Elections are the primary mechanism through which citizens in a democracy exercise political [power](../foundations/power-and-authority) and confer [legitimacy](../foundations/legitimacy) on their rulers. A voting system (or electoral system) is the set of rules determining how citizens cast ballots, how votes are tallied, and how seats are allocated. These rules directly influence parliamentary representation and the party landscape.

## Why it matters

Elections link citizens to political power. The voting system determines whether a legislature is dominated by two parties or split among many, whether regional and minority voices secure representation, and how closely the seat allocation reflects the popular vote. Understanding electoral rules is critical to assessing how representative a government truly is.

## How it works

### Major families of voting systems

**Plurality and majoritarian systems**

- **First-past-the-post (FPTP)**: voters choose one candidate per district; whoever wins the most votes takes the seat, even without an absolute majority. Used in the UK, the US, Canada, and India. FPTP often reinforces two-party dominance (Duverger's law) and can yield single-party majorities on a minority of the national vote.
- **Two-round system**: if no candidate wins an outright majority in round one, top candidates advance to a second round. Used in French presidential contests.

**Proportional representation (PR)**

- **Party-list PR**: voters back party lists, and legislative seats are distributed in proportion to each party's share of the vote. Common across continental Europe (e.g., the Netherlands, Sweden). It typically produces multi-party parliaments and coalition governance.
- **Mixed-member proportional (MMP)**: voters cast two votes — one for a local representative and one for a party list — with list seats allocated to ensure overall proportional results. Used in Germany and New Zealand.

**Ranked and mixed systems**

- **Single transferable vote (STV)**: voters rank candidates preferentially in multi-member districts, transferring surplus votes. Used in Ireland and the Australian Senate.
- **Parallel systems**: seats are divided between local districts and lists without compensatory proportionality, as in Japan.

### Requirements for genuine elections

Fair elections require more than balloting rules:

- **Universal suffrage**: equal voting rights for all adult citizens without discrimination, as outlined in the UDHR (Article 21) and ICCPR (Article 25).
- **Independent election administration**: non-partisan oversight of voter rolls and vote counting.
- **Open contestation**: reasonable ballot access without arbitrary disqualification of political opponents.

## Contested points

- **Which system is best?** FPTP advocates value stable governing majorities, accountability, and clear constituency ties. PR proponents emphasize seat proportionality, fair representation of minorities, and consensus politics. The choice involves fundamental trade-offs between stability and broad inclusion.
- **Are elections sufficient for democracy?** Holding elections does not by itself make a country democratic. Regimes that stage regular votes while suppressing independent media and banning viable opponents are categorized by scholars as "competitive authoritarianism" or hybrid regimes (Levitsky and Way, 2010).
- **Compulsory voting**: countries such as Australia and Belgium require citizens to vote by law. Proponents emphasize high turnout and civic duty; opponents see coerced participation as an infringement on personal liberty.

## Examples

- **United Kingdom, 2019**: under FPTP, the Conservative Party gained 56% of parliamentary seats with 44% of the popular vote, while the Liberal Democrats won only 2% of seats despite securing 12% of the vote.
- **Iran**: regular elections take place for the presidency and parliament (Majles), but the Guardian Council (شورای نگهبان) screens and disqualifies candidates before balloting. Critics view this screening as undermining genuine electoral competition, while supporters defend it as protecting constitutional standards.
- **New Zealand, 1996**: after voter frustration with disproportionate FPTP outcomes, New Zealand replaced FPTP with MMP following a referendum, shifting its legislature toward multi-party coalitions.
