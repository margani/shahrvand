## Definition

The separation of powers divides government functions among distinct branches — typically the legislature (which enacts laws), the executive (which enforces laws), and the judiciary (which interprets laws and resolves disputes). The purpose is to prevent any single authority from accumulating unchecked [power](../foundations/power-and-authority). It operates alongside checks and balances: mechanisms enabling each branch to constrain the others.

## Why it matters

Concentrated power poses risks of arbitrary rule, corruption, and abuse. The separation of powers provides a structural safeguard for individual [rights](../rights/rights-and-rule-of-law) and [the rule of law](../rights/rights-and-rule-of-law). Dividing governmental responsibilities fosters accountability by ensuring that independent branches can scrutinize and challenge one another's actions.

## How it works

### The three branches

| Branch | Primary function | Typical institution |
|---|---|---|
| **Legislature** | Enacts and amends laws, approves public budgets | Parliament, congress, national assembly |
| **Executive** | Implements laws, directs administration, conducts diplomacy | President, prime minister, cabinet |
| **Judiciary** | Adjudicates disputes, assesses constitutionality of state actions | Supreme court, constitutional tribunal |

### Institutional models

States structure the separation of powers in varying ways:

- **Presidential systems** (e.g., the United States, Brazil): the president is elected independently of the legislature as head of state and government. Executive and legislature possess separate mandates and cannot dissolve each other through ordinary votes.
- **Parliamentary systems** (e.g., the United Kingdom, Germany, India): the prime minister is chosen from and accountable to the legislature, subject to votes of no confidence. Executive and legislative functions are interlinked, while judicial independence is maintained.
- **Semi-presidential systems** (e.g., France): an elected president shares executive authority with a prime minister dependent on parliamentary support.
- **Hybrid systems**: Iran's system features an elected president and parliament, but the Supreme Leader holds ultimate constitutional authority above all branches, alongside oversight bodies like the Guardian Council.

### Checks and balances

Dividing functions is reinforced by reciprocal checks:

- **Executive veto**: the executive may reject legislative bills, subject to legislative override.
- **Judicial review**: courts may invalidate legislative or executive acts that breach the [constitution](constitutions).
- **Legislative oversight**: legislative committees investigate administrative agencies, confirm appointments, and allocate funding.
- **Impeachment**: legislatures may remove executives or judges for severe misconduct.

## Contested points

- **Efficiency vs. safeguards**: separation can cause institutional gridlock when branches disagree. Proponents argue gridlock prevents hasty, abusive policymaking, while critics argue it hinders decisive responses to crises.
- **Fusion vs. separation**: parliamentary systems intentionally fuse executive and legislative roles to avoid paralysis. Scholars debate whether this increases efficiency or weakens legislative oversight over cabinets.
- **Informal erosion**: formal separation can be weakened by strict party discipline, emergency powers, and partisan influence over judicial appointments.

## Examples

- **United States, 1787**: the U.S. Constitution established three distinct federal branches, influenced by Montesquieu's argument in *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748) that liberty cannot exist where legislative and executive powers unite.
- **Iran**: the constitution establishes legislative, executive, and judicial branches, but places the Supreme Leader above all three with overriding authority. The Guardian Council vets legislation, creating an institutional distribution distinct from classical tripartite models.
- **United Kingdom**: historically fused judicial and legislative powers in the House of Lords until the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 created an independent Supreme Court.
