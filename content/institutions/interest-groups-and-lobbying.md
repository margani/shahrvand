## Definition

An interest group (also termed an advocacy group, lobby, or pressure group) is an organized association of individuals or organizations formed around shared concerns, values, or economic interests that actively seeks to influence public policy, statutory law, and executive decisions. Unlike [political parties](political-parties), interest groups do not nominate candidates for office or seek to govern directly. Lobbying is the specific practice of directly communicating with legislators, executive officials, and regulators to persuade them to pass, amend, or reject specific laws, policies, or government contracts (Salisbury, 1969; Baumgartner and Leech, 1998).

## Why it matters

Interest groups are central actors in modern representative government, mediating between individual citizens and formal state institutions. They provide specialized technical data to legislative committees, highlight neglected social issues, monitor corporate and government conduct, and organize citizens outside of electoral cycles. However, lobbying also generates acute democratic controversies: when wealthy corporate lobbies outspend grassroots citizen organizations, political decision-making can be distorted in favor of private profit over public interest, eroding democratic [legitimacy](../foundations/legitimacy).

## How it works

### Typology of interest groups

Political scientists classify interest groups into two broad categories:

- **Sectional (economic) groups**: formed to protect and advance the tangible material or economic interests of their members. These include trade unions (e.g., AFL-CIO, CGT), business confederations (chambers of commerce), and professional syndicates (medical, legal, and engineering associations).
- **Cause (promotional) groups**: organized around shared moral, ideological, or public interest values, seeking collective benefits that do not exclusively enrich their own members. These include environmental organizations (Greenpeace), human rights watchdogs (Amnesty International), and gun rights or gun safety advocates.

### Methods of influence

Interest groups deploy varied tactics across political institutions:
- **Direct lobbying**: meeting legislators and executive bureaucrats to present expert research, draft proposed statutory language, and provide testimony during committee hearings.
- **Campaign finance and PACs**: funding political campaigns through Political Action Committees (PACs) or independent expenditure bodies, legally securing access to lawmakers (common in the United States).
- **Public mobilization (outside lobbying)**: organizing petition drives, media campaigns, and public demonstrations to influence voters and force legislators to respond.
- **Strategic litigation**: sponsoring legal challenges and submitting *amicus curiae* (friend of the court) briefs in supreme courts to invalidate unfavorable laws (see [Judiciary and courts](judiciary-and-courts)).

## Contested points

- **Pluralism vs. Elitism**: pluralist theorists (Dahl, 1961) argue that interest group competition is the lifeblood of democracy, ensuring that diverse social interests have a voice between elections and preventing any single faction from dominating. Elitist theorists (Mills, 1956; Schattschneider, 1960) counter that the pressure system is inherently skewed toward wealthy corporate interests, famously summarized by E. E. Schattschneider: "The flaw in the pluralist heaven is that the heavenly chorus sings with a strong upper-class accent."
- **The "Revolving Door"**: in many democracies, high-ranking government officials and legislators retire from office only to accept lucrative jobs with the very corporate lobbying firms they previously regulated. Critics argue this creates institutional conflicts of interest and regulatory capture, where agencies serve industry interests rather than the public.
- **Regulation and transparency**: international bodies advocate mandatory lobbying registries, disclosure of political spending, and cooling-off periods for former officials. Defenders of lobbying point out that the right to petition the government is a fundamental constitutional freedom (e.g., the First Amendment of the U.S. Constitution).

## Examples

- **United States**: the most formalized and financially intensive lobbying arena in the world; organizations like the National Rifle Association (NRA), the American Israel Public Affairs Committee (AIPAC), and the pharmaceutical lobby (PhRMA) deploy hundreds of millions of dollars to shape congressional votes and campaign elections.
- **European Union**: Brussels is home to over 12,000 registered interest groups seeking to influence European Commission directives and European Parliament regulations, subject to the EU Transparency Register.
- **Iran**: traditional commercial interest groups have historically exerted major political influence through the traditional Bazaar networks and guilds (*Asnaf*), whose strikes supported the 1906 Constitutional Revolution and the 1979 Islamic Revolution. In contemporary Iran, official trade unions are state-monitored (Workers' House / *Khaneh-ye Kargar*), while independent labor syndicates (such as the Vahed Bus Company union and Haft Tappeh sugar workers) operate without legal state recognition, facing persistent security scrutiny.
