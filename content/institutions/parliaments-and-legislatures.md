## Definition

A parliament or legislature is an assembly of elected (and occasionally appointed) representatives endowed with the constitutional authority to debate public policy, make and amend laws, and oversee government administration. As the deliberative branch of government, legislatures reflect the political diversity of the population and provide a formal forum where conflicting social interests are negotiated. Depending on their structure, legislatures are either unicameral (a single chamber) or bicameral (two chambers).

## Why it matters

Legislatures are the heartbeat of representative democracy. They ensure that laws are not dictated by executive fiat but result from open debate and compromise among representatives accountable to the electorate. Legislatures control the "power of the purse" — authorizing taxation and public expenditure — and possess critical investigative powers to prevent executive overreach and corruption.

## How it works

### Core functions of legislatures

Political scientists categorize legislative activities into five central functions (Packenham, 1970; Norton, 1990):

- **Legislation**: debating, drafting, amending, and voting on statutory laws that govern society.
- **Representation**: voicing the needs, values, and grievances of geographical constituencies, social groups, and ideological viewpoints.
- **Scrutiny and oversight**: questioning government ministers, conducting committee inquiries, evaluating state agency performance, and monitoring public spending.
- **Budgetary authorization**: approving the state budget, setting taxation limits, and ensuring public funds are properly expended.
- **Legitimation**: providing a transparent, procedural platform that confers legal and political [legitimacy](../foundations/legitimacy) on state policies.

### Unicameralism vs. Bicameralism

- **Unicameral legislatures** (e.g., Sweden, New Zealand, Iran's Majles): consist of a single chamber. They offer streamlined lawmaking, lower public costs, and clear, undiluted legislative accountability.
- **Bicameral legislatures** (e.g., the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany): consist of two chambers, typically a lower house directly elected by population size (e.g., the U.S. House of Representatives) and an upper house representing federal regions, states, or providing deliberate second-look scrutiny (e.g., the U.S. Senate or UK House of Lords). Bicameralism introduces checks and balances within the legislature itself, preventing hasty legislation.

## Contested points

- **Legislative dominance vs. Executive dominance**: in modern parliamentary systems, tight party discipline often enables the cabinet (executive) to dominate parliament, transforming the legislature into a reactive "rubber stamp" for government bills. Critics demand stronger independent legislative committee powers, while defenders argue strong executive leadership is essential for decisive governance.
- **Upper chamber legitimacy**: the democratic credentials of upper houses are frequently contested. In the UK, the House of Lords remains largely unelected (appointed life peers and hereditary peers), drawing continuous calls for reform. In the US, the Senate gives equal representation to every state regardless of population (Wyoming has the same two senators as California), prompting debates over whether this violates political equality.
- **Representative role: Delegate vs. Trustee**: Edmund Burke famously argued that a representative owes voters their mature judgment and conscience (the *trustee* model), not just obedience to constituent impulses. Conversely, proponents of the *delegate* model argue that legislators must strictly vote according to the clear preferences of the citizens who elected them.

## Examples

- **United Kingdom (Westminster Model)**: the "Mother of Parliaments," where the executive is drawn directly from the House of Commons, and weekly Prime Minister's Questions (PMQs) exemplifies direct parliamentary accountability.
- **United States Congress**: a powerful bicameral legislature with complete institutional independence from the president, possessing sweeping committee subpoena powers, budgetary supremacy, and the sole authority to declare war.
- **Iran (Majles)**: the Islamic Consultative Assembly (مجلس شورای اسلامی) is Iran's unicameral parliament. While it actively debates legislation, investigates ministries, and votes on cabinet confirmations, its laws become binding only after approval by the Guardian Council, which reviews all legislation for conformity with the constitution and Islamic jurisprudence.
