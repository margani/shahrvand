## Definition

A party system is the network of relationships and competition among [political parties](political-parties) operating within a state. It is defined not merely by how many parties exist, but by their size, ideological distance, and how they interact to form governments. Political scientists categorize both individual parties (by their social base and structure) and the broader party systems within which they compete.

## Why it matters

The structure of a party system directly affects political stability, policy continuity, and legislative representation. A system with two dominant parties forces broad compromises before elections, whereas a fragmented multi-party system negotiates compromises afterward during coalition talks. Understanding party systems explains why elections in different countries lead to distinct governing outcomes, from stable one-party cabinets to volatile multi-party coalitions.

## How it works

### Typology of political parties

Historically, political scientists classify parties into several organisational models (Duverger, 1954; Kirchheimer, 1966):

- **Cadre (elite) parties**: early parliamentary groupings formed around notable individuals and elites with limited popular membership, common in 19th-century Europe.
- **Mass parties**: formed in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, mobilizing large working-class or religious memberships who paid regular dues (e.g., European social-democratic parties).
- **Catch-all parties**: emergent after World War II, de-emphasizing rigid class ideologies to appeal to broad cross-sections of the voting public (Kirchheimer, 1966).
- **Cartel parties**: modern parties that rely increasingly on state funding and access to public media rather than grassroots membership, forming an intertwined relationship with the state machinery (Katz and Mair, 1995).

### Major types of party systems

Political scientist Giovanni Sartori (1976) developed the foundational framework classifying party systems:

- **Dominant-party systems**: one party regularly wins elections and controls government over decades despite open competition (e.g., the Liberal Democratic Party in Japan for much of the post-1955 era; the African National Congress in South Africa since 1994).
- **Two-party systems**: two major parties dominate elections and alternate in power, typically encouraged by first-past-the-post [voting rules](elections-and-voting-systems) (e.g., the United States and the United Kingdom).
- **Moderate multi-party systems**: between three and five main parties compete, generally forming moderate coalitions near the political center (e.g., Germany, the Netherlands).
- **Polarized multi-party systems**: numerous parties compete across wide ideological chasms, often featuring anti-system or radical fringes that make consensus difficult.
- **Single-party (monopoly) systems**: only one ruling party is legally permitted or possesses monopolistic state control, characteristic of authoritarian and totalitarian states (e.g., the Communist Party of China).

## Contested points

- **Are two-party systems more stable than multi-party systems?** Proponents of two-party systems argue they deliver decisive election outcomes, clear accountability, and prevent radical fringe parties from holding disproportionate leverage. Advocates of multi-party systems counter that two-party competition polarizes public discourse and leaves voters with restrictive choices, whereas proportional multi-party systems foster consensus and compromise.
- **Does electoral law determine the party system?** Duverger's Law (1954) famously states that single-member district plurality systems favour a two-party format, while proportional representation fosters multi-party diversity. Critics argue that social divisions (ethnic, linguistic, regional, and class cleavages) are equally influential in determining the number of parties that emerge.
- **Are political parties becoming obsolete?** Some observers argue that direct social movements, digital activism, and populist figures bypassing party bureaucracies demonstrate that traditional parties are losing their relevance. Defenders point out that lawmaking, executive oversight, and stable policy formulation still require institutional party discipline.

## Examples

- **United States**: a classic two-party system structured by single-member districts and direct primaries, where the Democratic and Republican parties have monopolized national politics since the mid-19th century.
- **Germany**: a moderate multi-party system shaped by mixed-member proportional representation, where parties such as the Christian Democrats (CDU/CSU) and Social Democrats (SPD) regularly govern through formal coalition agreements with smaller parties (FDP, Greens).
- **Iran**: under Mohammad Reza Shah, Iran shifted from a competitive royal-monitored system to a mandatory single-party regime with the creation of the Rastakhiz (Resurgence) Party in 1975, which dissolved all legal opposition parties until the 1979 revolution.
