## Definition

Federalism and unitary systems are the two primary constitutional architectures governing the territorial distribution of political [power](power-and-authority) within a [state](../foundations/the-state). In a **federal system**, sovereignty is constitutionally divided between a central (national) government and subnational constituent units (states, provinces, or cantons), such that neither tier can unilaterally abolish the other or usurp its constitutionally entrenched powers. In a **unitary state**, supreme legal authority resides exclusively in the central national government; while regional and local bodies may exercise delegated administrative duties (devolution), their powers exist solely at the discretion of the national parliament and can be modified or revoked by ordinary law (Watts, 2008; Riker, 1964).

## Why it matters

Territorial governance determines how a country accommodates ethnic, linguistic, and cultural diversity, distributes natural resource wealth, and preserves democratic stability. In geographically vast or culturally heterogeneous nations, federalism allows distinct regional communities to govern their local affairs autonomously while maintaining a unified national economy and defense. Conversely, unitary systems offer administrative consistency, legal uniformity, and equal public standards across the entirety of a country.

## How it works

### Structural components of a federation

Political scientists identify five institutional hallmarks common to genuine federal systems (Watts, 2008):

- **A written constitution with entrenched powers**: the division of jurisdictional authority (e.g., defense to the center; education and policing to the regions) is codified in supreme law and cannot be amended without subnational consent.
- **Constitutional arbitration**: an independent supreme or constitutional court with the authority to resolve jurisdictional disputes between the central government and regional states (see [Judiciary and courts](judiciary-and-courts)).
- **Bicameral legislature**: an upper legislative chamber (e.g., the U.S. Senate, the German Bundesrat) specifically representing regional states, providing regions with a veto over national policies.
- **Fiscal federalism**: regional units possess independent taxing authority to fund their responsibilities without total financial dependence on the center.
- **Symmetrical vs. Asymmetrical federalism**: symmetrical federations grant identical powers to all constituent units (e.g., the United States); asymmetrical federations grant special linguistic, cultural, or legal powers to specific regions to accommodate distinct national minorities (e.g., Quebec in Canada, Catalonia in Spain).

### Devolution in unitary states

Unitary states are not necessarily centralized dictatorships. Many established unitary democracies utilize **devolution** — statutory decentralization where a national legislature grants regional parliaments the authority to make local laws (e.g., the Scottish Parliament and Welsh Senedd in the United Kingdom). However, under constitutional law, Westminster retains absolute parliamentary sovereignty and could theoretically revoke devolved powers.

## Contested points

- **Unity vs. Secession**: proponents argue federalism preserves multi-ethnic states by accommodating minority self-rule peacefully. Critics counter that institutionalizing regional and ethnic identities provides local leaders with state machinery, budgets, and flags that can facilitate nationalist secession and eventual state collapse (Roeder, 2007).
- **Policy experimentation vs. Inequality**: federalism enables "laboratories of democracy," where subnational units experiment with novel social and economic policies (e.g., environmental standards or healthcare programs) before national adoption. Critics respond that federalism produces severe inequalities in civil rights, education quality, and public healthcare depending on where citizens reside (the "postcode lottery").
- **Efficiency vs. Duplication**: unitary systems avoid the expensive bureaucratic duplication, intergovernmental disputes, and conflicting regional regulations inherent in federal arrangements, enabling swifter, coordinated national responses to crises.

## Examples

- **United States**: one of the world's oldest federations (1787); powers not explicitly granted to the federal government by the Constitution are reserved to the states (Tenth Amendment), resulting in major interstate variations in criminal law, taxation, and voting rules.
- **Germany**: a cooperative federal system (*Bund-Länder*) where the federal government enacts overarching statutory framework laws, but the sixteen regional states (*Länder*) administer them and exercise direct legislative power through the Bundesrat.
- **United Kingdom and France**: classic unitary states. France maintains a unified, centralized administrative structure governed from Paris. The UK combines central parliamentary supremacy with asymmetrical devolution to Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland.
- **Iran**: constitutionally structured as a centralized unitary state. Executive governors (*ostandaran*) and local administrative leaders are appointed by the Ministry of Interior in Tehran. While Article 100 of the Constitution provides for elected local Islamic City and Village Councils (*Showraha*), their powers are strictly confined to municipal public works and local health/education oversight, remaining subordinate to central state ministries.
