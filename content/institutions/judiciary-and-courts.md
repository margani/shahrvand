## Definition

The judiciary is the system of courts and judges that interprets and applies the law in the name of the [state](../foundations/the-state). It resolves legal conflicts between private individuals, businesses, and government bodies, and determines guilt and penalties in criminal proceedings. Judicial independence is the foundational institutional principle that judges must decide cases impartially, guided strictly by the law and facts, free from external influence, political pressure, or intimidation from the executive, the legislature, or private parties.

## Why it matters

Without an independent judiciary, the [rule of law](../rights/rights-and-rule-of-law) and constitutional [rights](../rights/rights-and-rule-of-law) are illusions. If the government can pressure courts, laws become instruments of arbitrary political power: dissidents are silenced without fair trials, property is seized unlawfully, and electoral malpractice goes unpunished. Independent courts ensure that every person — including presidents, prime ministers, and security officials — remains subordinate to the law.

## How it works

### Core functions of the judicial branch

The judiciary fulfills three essential constitutional roles (Guarnieri and Pederzoli, 2002; Shapiro, 1981):

- **Dispute adjudication**: impartially resolving civil controversies and determining criminal liability based on established statutory and common law.
- **Constitutional and administrative review**: evaluating whether actions by the executive and laws passed by the legislature comply with the [constitution](constitutions).
- **Protecting fundamental rights**: serving as an institutional shield guarding individual citizens against unconstitutional state overreach.

### Safeguards for judicial independence

International legal instruments, such as the UN Basic Principles on the Independence of the Judiciary (1985), specify structural safeguards necessary to insulate judges:
- **Security of tenure**: judges cannot be dismissed or demoted arbitrarily by political leaders before retirement, except through rigorous disciplinary procedures for gross misconduct.
- **Financial autonomy**: judicial salaries and court budgets must be legally protected from executive retaliation or arbitrary reduction.
- **Independent appointments**: transparent appointment procedures involving independent judicial councils or cross-party confirmation, preventing purely partisan court-packing.
- **Immunity for official acts**: protection from civil lawsuits or criminal prosecution for decisions made in their judicial capacity.

## Contested points

- **Judicial activism vs. Judicial restraint**: a major controversy surrounds how judges interpret legal texts. Proponents of judicial restraint argue unelected judges must strictly defer to the plain text and the will of elected legislatures. Advocates of judicial activism (or the "living constitution" approach) argue courts must adapt broad constitutional principles to modern realities, protecting vulnerable minorities against majoritarian indifference.
- **Accountability vs. Independence**: while judges must be independent of politics, complete lack of accountability risks creating an insular, self-serving judicial elite. Designing mechanisms that discipline corrupt or incompetent judges without compromising their decision-making freedom is a persistent institutional challenge.
- **Politicization of judicial appointments**: in many nations, the process of selecting supreme court judges has become an intense partisan battleground, leading critics to question whether high courts operate as neutral legal arbiters or unelected political actors.

## Examples

- **United States**: the federal judiciary possesses strong constitutional independence; judges are nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate for lifetime tenure during "good Behaviour," establishing profound institutional autonomy (*Marbury v. Madison*, 1803).
- **South Africa**: the Constitutional Court, created in 1994, has delivered landmark judgments affirming socioeconomic rights, holding executive presidents accountable, and striking down discriminatory legislation.
- **Iran**: the judiciary (قوه قضائیه) is established as an independent branch under Article 156 of the Constitution. However, the Head of the Judiciary is appointed directly for five-year terms by the Supreme Leader, rather than through legislative or peer selection. Revolutionary Courts (دادگاه‌های انقلاب) operate alongside public courts, handling national security, political offenses, and economic crimes under specialized procedures that international legal bodies have repeatedly scrutinized regarding fair trial guarantees.
