## Definition

The military and politics (civil-military relations) encompasses the institutional relationships, constitutional balances, and distribution of [power](../foundations/power-and-authority) between the armed forces of a [state](../foundations/the-state) and its civilian political leadership. As the institution possessing an organized monopoly on lethal violence, the military is essential for external territorial defense and national security. The central dilemma of civil-military relations, formulated classically by Roman satirist Juvenal as *Quis custodiet ipsos custodes?* ("Who guards the guardians?"), is how to create a military strong enough to protect the state while preventing it from dominating or overthrowing civilian government (Huntington, 1957; Finer, 1962).

## Why it matters

Throughout modern history, military intervention in politics — through coups d'état, military juntas, and covert intelligence surveillance — has been the single most frequent cause of democratic breakdown. When soldiers govern, civil liberties, the [rule of law](../rights/rights-and-rule-of-law), and parliamentary accountability are systematically suspended. Conversely, establishing firm, democratic civilian oversight of armed forces and security agencies is a non-negotiable prerequisite for consolidated constitutional democracy.

## How it works

### Samuel Huntington's models of civilian control

In *The Soldier and the State* (1957), political scientist Samuel P. Huntington identified two distinct models of civilian control over the military:

- **Subjective civilian control**: maximizing the power of specific civilian groups, political parties, or classes over the military. Under this model, the military is thoroughly politicized, with officer loyalty secured through ideological indoctrination, political party vetting, or ethnic favoritism (common in totalitarian, revolutionary, and authoritarian states).
- **Objective civilian control**: maximizing military *professionalism*. The officer corps is cultivated as an autonomous, politically neutral, disciplined profession with specialized military expertise, remaining completely detached from partisan politics and willingly subordinate to whatever legitimate civilian government holds constitutional office.

### Forms of military political intervention

Political scientist S. E. Finer (1962) outlined a spectrum of military political involvement:
1. **Constitutional influence**: offering professional defense advice to civilian ministers through regular institutional channels.
2. **Pressuring civilians**: threatening non-cooperation or resignation to force specific foreign or defense policies.
3. **Displacement (veto power)**: threatening or executing a coup to remove an elected leader, replacing them with handpicked civilian puppets.
4. **Direct military rule (Junta)**: seizing total executive power directly, suspending the [constitution](constitutions), dissolving parliament, and placing generals at the head of government ministries.

## Contested points

- **Professionalism vs. Interventionism**: Huntington argued that increasing military professionalism inherently prevents coups. Critics (Finer, 1962; Janowitz, 1960) counter that highly professionalized officer corps often develop an intense corporate identity, viewing themselves as the "ultimate guardians of the nation" against "corrupt civilian politicians," intervening precisely because of their professional convictions.
- **The military-industrial complex**: in established democracies, critics worry about the subtle political influence of the defense industry, retired generals, and defense contractors. President Dwight D. Eisenhower warned in his 1961 Farewell Address against the "acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex."
- **Internal security and militarized policing**: deploying military units for domestic law enforcement or crowd control blurs the boundary between external warfare and domestic civil rights, raising severe risks of civilian casualties and constitutional erosion.

## Examples

- **Turkey (1960–1997)**: the Turkish military established itself as the constitutional guardian of secular Kemalist principles, launching direct or indirect coups in 1960, 1971, 1980, and 1997 ("postmodern coup") whenever elected civilian governments were deemed too Islamist or unstable.
- **Latin America (1960s–1980s)**: widespread military coups established bureaucratic-authoritarian juntas across Brazil (1964), Chile (1973 under Pinochet), and Argentina (1976), justifying brutal political repression through the "National Security Doctrine."
- **Iran**: modern Iranian history is profoundly shaped by the armed forces: the 1921 coup brought Reza Khan to power, transforming the army into the backbone of Pahlavi state-building; the 1953 coup d'état overthrew Prime Minister Mohammad Mossadegh. Following the 1979 Revolution, the new leadership created the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC / سپاه پاسداران) alongside the regular army (*Artesh*). The IRGC operates as an ideologically driven military institution with sweeping economic foundations, foreign intelligence operations, and major domestic political influence.
