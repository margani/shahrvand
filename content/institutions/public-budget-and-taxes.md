## Definition

A public budget is a comprehensive, legally binding financial plan enacted annually by the legislature that authorizes the government to collect revenues (principally through taxation) and allocate expenditures across public functions (defense, healthcare, infrastructure, education, and social protection). Taxation is the compulsory transfer of financial resources from private individuals and corporations to the [state](../foundations/the-state), enforced by law without direct quid-pro-quo exchange. The legislative authority over taxation and expenditure is traditionally termed the "power of the purse" (Musgrave, 1959; Wildavsky, 1964).

## Why it matters

The budget is the single most important policy statement a government produces each year; it reveals the actual, operational priorities of a state far more reliably than campaign speeches or constitutional preambles. Historically, the struggle over taxation was the primary catalyst that forced autocratic monarchs to grant representation to citizens, crystallizing the revolutionary democratic demand: "No taxation without representation." How a state taxes and spends determines the level of economic inequality, the stability of the currency, and the quality of public goods available to ordinary people.

## How it works

### Core functions of public finance

Economist Richard Musgrave formulated the foundational three-branch framework of fiscal policy (Musgrave, 1959):

- **Allocation**: providing public goods that the private market underproduces or cannot provide efficiently (national defense, justice systems, basic scientific research, environmental protection).
- **Distribution (Redistribution)**: using progressive taxation and social transfers (welfare, pensions, public healthcare) to reduce market-generated inequalities and poverty.
- **Stabilization**: deploying fiscal policy (adjusting government spending and borrowing) to manage macroeconomic cycles, prevent runaway inflation, and combat mass unemployment.

### The annual budgetary cycle

In constitutional democracies, the public budget proceeds through four mandatory stages (Wildavsky, 1964):
1. **Executive drafting**: ministries compile spending estimates coordinated by the finance ministry.
2. **Legislative scrutiny and enactment**: [parliaments](parliaments-and-legislatures) interrogate ministers, amend appropriations, and formally vote to pass the budget into law.
3. **Execution**: administrative agencies disburse allocated funds according to statutory mandates.
4. **Audit and oversight**: independent supreme audit institutions (such as the Government Accountability Office in the US or Courts of Audit in Europe) inspect public accounts to detect waste, fraud, and illegal expenditure.

## Contested points

- **Progressive vs. Flat taxation**: advocates of progressive taxation argue that those with higher incomes have a greater ability to pay and benefit disproportionately from state-secured legal and commercial infrastructure, justifying higher marginal tax rates to fund public services. Proponents of flat taxes or supply-side economics argue that high tax rates punish entrepreneurship, reduce capital investment, and encourage tax evasion.
- **Deficits and public debt**: Keynesian economists argue that governments should borrow and spend during economic downturns to stimulate demand and protect employment. Fiscal conservatives counter that chronic deficit spending accumulates unsustainable public debt burdens on future generations, fuels inflation, and crowds out private borrowing.
- **Rentier states and the "Resource Curse"**: political scientists observe that governments funded primarily by oil, gas, or mineral royalties rather than citizen taxes (the *rentier state* model) often become unresponsive and autocratic; when rulers do not need citizens' tax money, they do not need to grant them representation or institutional accountability (Ross, 2001).

## Examples

- **English Civil War and Bill of Rights (1689)**: King Charles I's attempts to levy taxes without parliamentary approval precipitated the Civil War; the 1689 Bill of Rights explicitly established that levying money for the Crown without grant of Parliament is illegal, enshrining the principle of legislative budgetary control.
- **The Nordic social model (Sweden, Denmark)**: maintains high overall tax burdens (averaging over 40% of GDP) with progressive income taxes and value-added taxes, financing universal free childcare, healthcare, free higher education, and comprehensive pension systems.
- **Iran**: exemplifies the dynamics of an oil-dependent rentier budget. Oil and gas revenues have historically financed large state subsidies, a vast civil service, and security expenditures. Fluctuations in international oil prices and comprehensive international economic sanctions have produced severe fiscal deficits, driving reliance on money printing, high inflation, and volatile debates in the Majles over subsidy cuts and budget transparency.
