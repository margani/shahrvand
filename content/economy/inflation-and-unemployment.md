---
title: Inflation, unemployment and politics
description: How macroeconomic volatility, price instability, and joblessness shape political legitimacy and electoral outcomes.
---

## Definition
Inflation and unemployment are the two central macroeconomic indicators that directly affect the daily material well-being of citizens. Inflation denotes a sustained, broad-based increase in the general price level of goods and services, resulting in a progressive erosion of the purchasing power of money. Unemployment measures the share of the economically active labour force that is actively seeking work but unable to find employment.

In political science, the interaction between these indicators is frequently encapsulated in the "misery index" (the sum of the unemployment rate and the inflation rate), which serves as a powerful empirical predictor of incumbent electoral vulnerability and social unrest.

## Why it matters
Few economic phenomena destabilize political regimes as rapidly as high inflation and mass joblessness. Severe inflation destroys the value of lifetime savings, reduces real wages, and causes acute social panic by rendering daily subsistence unpredictable. Mass unemployment deprives individuals of income, dignity, and purpose, leading to despair, higher crime rates, and the erosion of civic trust.

Consequently, voters routinely punish governing parties during periods of economic distress, attributing macroeconomic misery to government incompetence. In extreme cases, hyperinflation or catastrophic unemployment has destroyed constitutional orders, creating conditions for the rise of authoritarian and totalitarian dictatorships.

## How it works
The political dynamics of inflation and unemployment operate through structural economic policy tools:

1. **Monetary Policy and Central Bank Independence**: Independent central banks raise interest rates to cool down an overheating economy and suppress inflation. However, higher interest rates intentionally slow economic activity, often increasing unemployment. Politicians often pressure central banks to keep interest rates artificially low before elections to boost short-term growth (political business cycles).
2. **Fiscal Policy**: Governments use taxation and deficit spending to influence aggregate demand. Keynesian fiscal stimulus can reduce unemployment during recessions, but excessive deficit spending without productive capacity can trigger demand-pull inflation.
3. **Supply-Side Shocks and Cost-Push Inflation**: External shocks—such as energy price spikes, wars, supply chain breakdowns, or international trade sanctions—drive up production costs independently of domestic demand.
4. **The Phillips Curve Dilemma**: Historically, policymakers perceived a trade-off between unemployment and inflation: reducing unemployment risked higher inflation, while fighting inflation required accepting job losses. In the 1970s, "stagflation" (simultaneous high inflation and stagnation) challenged this assumption.
5. **Distributional Winners and Losers**: Inflation transfers wealth from creditors and fixed-income savers to debtors (including governments with high sovereign debt). Unemployment disproportionately strikes young, lower-skilled, and minority workers.

## Contested points
The political responsibility for managing inflation and unemployment generates fundamental disputes between economic schools of thought.

Monetarists and orthodox economists, following Milton Friedman, assert that "inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." They argue that inflation is caused exclusively by excessive central bank money creation. They advocate for strict central bank independence, legally mandated inflation targets (typically 2%), fiscal austerity, and labour market deregulation, arguing that unemployment cannot be pushed below its "natural rate" without igniting runaway inflation.

Conversely, post-Keynesian and heterodox economists argue that modern inflation is frequently driven by corporate profit-gouging ("greedflation"), supply chain bottlenecks, and energy cartels rather than government spending or wage increases. They advocate for targeted strategic price controls, windfall profit taxes on energy monopolies, and public job guarantees, arguing that using deliberate unemployment to discipline inflation is economically wasteful and morally cruel.

## Examples
The classic historical warning of hyperinflation occurred during the Weimar Republic in Germany in 1923. Massive post-war reparations and government money-printing triggered runaway hyperinflation where a loaf of bread cost billions of marks, wiping out the middle class and fatally undermining trust in parliamentary democracy, paving the path toward Nazism during the Great Depression.

In the United States, the stagflation of the late 1970s crippled the presidency of Jimmy Carter, facilitating the 1980 election of Ronald Reagan. Federal Reserve Chairman Paul Volcker dramatically raised the federal funds rate to 20% in 1981, inducing a painful recession with unemployment exceeding 10% in order to break inflationary expectations.

In Iran, chronic high inflation (frequently exceeding 40% annually) and structural youth unemployment have shaped political instability for decades. Caused by high budget deficits financed by Central Bank money printing, structural banking imbalances, and compounded by international sanctions, persistent inflation has decimated the purchasing power of the middle and working classes. In November 2019, a sudden tripling of subsidized gasoline prices triggered nationwide protests, which were met with severe state violence, underscoring how volatile economic grievances ignite direct political confrontation.
