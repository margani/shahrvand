## Definition
Economic inequality refers to the unequal distribution of financial assets, wealth, income, and opportunities across individuals and demographic groups within a society or between nations. Income inequality measures the disparity in earnings received from wages, investments, and government transfers over a specific period, whereas wealth inequality measures disparities in accumulated net assets, including real estate, corporate equities, and savings.

Economists and political scientists commonly quantify inequality using the Gini coefficient—a statistical measure ranging from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (maximal inequality)—as well as income-share ratios, such as the share of national income captured by the top 1% or top 10% of households.

## Why it matters
The degree of economic inequality profoundly structures the distribution of political power and social cohesion. When wealth becomes excessively concentrated, affluent elites can translate financial leverage into political dominance through campaign financing, media ownership, and policy lobbying, effectively creating an oligarchy behind a democratic facade.

Severe inequality impedes intergenerational social mobility, restricts educational opportunities for children from disadvantaged backgrounds, and fosters geographic segregation. At a systemic level, excessive inequality depresses aggregate consumer demand, precipitates recurrent financial crises, and fuels societal resentment, which frequently empowers polarizing populist movements.

## How it works
Economic inequality is driven and reproduced through multiple intersecting mechanisms:

1. **Capital Accumulation vs. Economic Growth**: As economist Thomas Piketty demonstrated, when the rate of return on capital (r) systematically exceeds the overall economic growth rate (g), inherited wealth inevitably concentrates faster than wages rise.
2. **Technological Change and Globalisation**: Skill-biased technological change increases wages for highly educated professionals while automating routine manufacturing and service jobs. Offshoring and trade competition have similarly suppressed manufacturing wages in advanced economies.
3. **Labour Market Institutions**: The secular decline of trade union density, statutory minimum wages lagging behind inflation, and the proliferation of precarious gig-economy contracts have weakened worker bargaining leverage.
4. **Tax and Transfer Policies**: The shift toward less progressive income tax codes, reductions in corporate tax rates, and the erosion or abolition of inheritance and wealth taxes have weakened the state's redistributive capacity.
5. **Intergenerational Wealth Transmission**: Unequal inheritances, disparities in access to elite educational networks, and residential segregation ensure that class advantages persist across generations.

## Contested points
The normative evaluation of inequality and the appropriate governmental remedies remain among the most contested debates in contemporary politics.

Free-market liberals argue that a degree of income inequality is an unavoidable and beneficial feature of a dynamic economy. In this view, disparate financial rewards reflect differences in talent, effort, educational investment, and entrepreneurial risk-taking. Policies that aggressively compress income distributions, they argue, stifle innovation, penalise success, and reduce overall economic output. They contend that policymakers should focus exclusively on reducing absolute poverty and expanding equality of opportunity rather than limiting inequality of outcome.

Conversely, egalitarian philosophers and left-leaning economists argue that genuine equality of opportunity is impossible in a society with extreme inequality of outcome, as children inherit vast material disparities at birth. They assert that extreme concentrations of wealth threaten the basic equality of citizenship essential to democracy. Proponents advocate for redistributive mechanisms, including wealth taxes, progressive inheritance taxes, stronger collective bargaining, and universal public goods.

## Examples
The Gilded Age in late-19th-century America exemplified extreme inequality, where corporate monopolists (such as John D. Rockefeller and J.P. Morgan) accumulated unprecedented personal fortunes while urban factory workers lived in squalor. This disparity catalyzed the Progressive Era, leading to antitrust laws and the ratification of the Sixteenth Amendment in 1913, which established the federal income tax.

In recent decades, the World Inequality Report has documented sharp increases in wealth concentration globally. In the United States, the share of total net wealth owned by the top 1% surged from roughly 23% in the late 1970s to over 32% by the 2020s, accompanied by wage stagnation for the bottom 50%.

In Iran, economic inequality has been aggravated by currency depreciation, chronic inflation, and rentier allocation. According to official data from the Statistical Centre of Iran, the Gini coefficient has consistently hovered near or above 0.40 over the past decade. The distribution of wealth reflects an acute cleavage between a politically connected economic elite (*aghazadehs*) benefiting from preferential exchange rates and import monopolies, and millions of working-class families pushed below the absolute poverty line, sparking repeated nationwide economic strikes and protests.
