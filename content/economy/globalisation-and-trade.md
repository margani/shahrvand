title: Globalisation and trade
description: The political drivers, economic institutions, and distributional conflicts of cross-border trade and financial integration.

## Definition
Globalisation and trade refers to the deepening interconnectedness and interdependence of the world's economies, societies, and political systems across national borders. In economics, it manifests primarily through international trade (the cross-border exchange of goods and services), foreign direct investment, cross-border capital flows, global production value chains, and the migration of labour.

While cross-border trade has ancient antecedents, contemporary economic globalisation is structured by multilateral legal institutions, tariff reductions, supply chain containerization, and digital communications that enable the fragmentation of manufacturing across continents.

## Why it matters
The expansion of global trade over the post-WWII era has dramatically restructured international politics and domestic economies. Globalisation enabled developing nations—most notably in East Asia—to integrate into global supply chains, lifting more than one billion people out of extreme poverty. International trade enhances consumer choice and lowers the prices of food, electronics, and manufactured goods worldwide.

Concurrently, globalisation has destabilised domestic social contracts. The offshoring of industrial manufacturing dismantled working-class communities in advanced industrial democracies, generating deep economic grievance and feeding nationalist and protectionist political backlash. Global economic interdependence also creates systemic vulnerabilities, as seen in global financial contagion, critical supply chain dependencies, and geopolitical weaponisation of trade.

## How it works
The architecture of global trade operates across multilateral, regional, and national layers:

1. **Comparative Advantage**: Classical trade theory (David Ricardo) posits that nations maximize collective wealth by specializing in producing goods where their opportunity cost is lowest, trading for other necessities.
2. **Global Value Chains (GVCs)**: Modern industrial products (such as smartphones or automobiles) are rarely made in a single country; components are designed, fabricated, assembled, and marketed across dozens of nations.
3. **Multilateral Governance and Rules**: The World Trade Organization (WTO), established in 1995, sets legally binding global trade rules, enforces tariff commitments, and adjudicates trade disputes through its dispute settlement mechanism.
4. **Regional Trade Agreements (RTAs)**: Blocs such as the European Union (EU single market), the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA), and ASEAN eliminate tariffs among member states and harmonize technical standards.
5. **Trade Policy Instruments**: States manage trade through tariffs (import taxes), non-tariff barriers (quotas, environmental standards, phytosanitary rules), export controls, and industrial subsidies.

## Contested points
The political benefits and socioeconomic costs of free trade generate intense polarization between cosmopolitan free-traders and economic nationalists.

Proponents of free trade, rooted in neoliberal economics, argue that removing trade barriers maximizes economic efficiency, enhances global competition, spurs technological diffusion, and fosters peaceful international relations by making war economically ruinous (the "capitalist peace" thesis). They argue that protectionism protects uncompetitive domestic monopolies at the expense of ordinary consumers.

Conversely, critics across the political spectrum challenge the free-trade orthodoxy. Left-wing critics and dependency theorists argue that corporate globalisation promotes a "race to the bottom" in wages, worker protections, and environmental regulations, empowering multinational capital to escape domestic taxation while impoverishing the Global South through unequal terms of trade.

Right-wing and realist economic nationalists emphasize national security and strategic autonomy. They argue that excessive interdependence leaves nations vulnerable to geopolitical coercion, supply interruptions during international conflicts, and the catastrophic loss of domestic industrial and technological capacity to strategic rivals.

## Examples
The creation of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) in 1947 and its evolution into the World Trade Organization (WTO) in 1995 formed the bedrock of post-war global trade. The accession of the People's Republic of China to the WTO in December 2001 accelerated global manufacturing integration, creating the "China Shock" that reorganized global manufacturing employment.

The political backlash against hyper-globalisation culminated in 2016 with the United Kingdom's vote to leave the European Union (Brexit) and the election of Donald Trump in the United States, who subsequently imposed sweeping tariffs on steel, aluminum, and hundreds of billions of dollars of Chinese imports, marking a shift toward aggressive economic nationalism and industrial reshoring.

In Iran, integration into global trade has been constrained by ideology, state monopolisation, and comprehensive sanctions. Following the 1979 Revolution, the state pursued economic self-sufficiency (*khodkafaei*). However, Iran remains critically dependent on international markets for capital goods, industrial machinery, and consumer staples, financed by crude oil exports. Being excluded from the WTO and facing secondary financial sanctions, Iran has increasingly pivoted toward non-Western regional trade, entering long-term strategic arrangements with China and joining the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) and BRICS.
