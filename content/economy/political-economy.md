## Definition
Political economy is the interdisciplinary study of how political institutions, power relationships, and the legal environment interact with economic systems, markets, and the distribution of wealth and resources. Originating in the moral philosophy of Adam Smith, David Ricardo, and Karl Marx, it examines how politics shapes economic outcomes and how economic interests influence political decisions.

Rather than treating the economy as a self-regulating, autonomous sphere governed purely by mathematical laws, political economy investigates how property rights, tax policies, labour laws, and trade regulations are created and contested by political actors possessing unequal leverage and resources.

## Why it matters
Every major political decision carries profound economic consequences, and every economic policy reflects underlying political choices. Decisions regarding tax rates, public infrastructure investment, environmental regulations, and central bank interest rates directly determine who prospers, who bears economic risk, and how wealth is distributed across society.

Understanding political economy allows citizens to demystify technical economic jargon and recognize that market structures are not inevitable natural phenomena, but political constructs. It illuminates how economic inequality can translate into political dominance, enabling wealthy elites to shape electoral rules and public policy in their own favour.

## How it works
Political economy analyzes society through several intersecting frameworks:

1. **Institutions and Rules**: The legal rules that govern markets—including contract enforcement, bankruptcy laws, patent protections, and labour standards—are established and enforced by state power.
2. **Distributional Conflict**: Every public policy creates winners and losers. Free trade agreements may lower consumer prices and benefit export corporations while harming domestic manufacturing workers. Political economy tracks how these competing groups mobilize to influence legislation.
3. **The State and the Market**: Analyzing the degree of government intervention in the economy, ranging from laissez-faire capitalism to state-directed developmental models and planned economies.
4. **Rent-Seeking and Public Choice**: Examining how special-interest groups and corporations use political influence, lobbying, and campaign donations to secure subsidies, regulatory exemptions, or monopolies (economic rents) at the expense of the public.
5. **International Political Economy (IPE)**: Studying how global trade, international finance, multinational corporations, and institutions like the IMF and World Bank interact with state sovereignty and geopolitical power.

## Contested points
The relationship between state authority and market dynamics is the central ideological fault line in political economy.

Free-market liberals, inspired by thinkers like Friedrich Hayek and Milton Friedman, argue that decentralized market mechanisms maximize individual freedom, innovation, and productive efficiency. They assert that government intervention inevitably distorts price signals, introduces bureaucratic inefficiencies, and encourages corrupt rent-seeking.

In contrast, social democrats and Keynesians argue that unconstrained markets inherently generate severe market failures, including monopoly power, economic inequality, environmental degradation, and cyclical financial crises. They contend that robust state intervention—through public healthcare, progressive taxation, strong welfare nets, and strategic industrial policy—is necessary to stabilize markets and ensure human dignity.

From a Marxist and critical perspective, capitalist markets are rooted in class exploitation, where capital owners extract surplus value from wage labour. In this view, the state is not a neutral arbiter, but an instrument that enforces property rights and stabilizes the capitalist system.

## Examples
The Great Depression of the 1930s sparked a profound transformation in political economy. The catastrophic collapse of unregulated financial markets led to the implementation of the New Deal in the United States and the widespread adoption of Keynesian economics across the West. Governments embraced deficit spending, established state-backed social security systems, and implemented stringent financial regulations (such as the Glass-Steagall Act of 1933) to stabilize capitalism through public management.

In East Asia, the "Developmental State" model in post-war Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan demonstrated how state bureaucracy could actively guide market capitalism. Through state-directed credit, selective tariffs, and targeted investments in export-oriented industrialisation, these governments orchestrated rapid economic transformations while maintaining private corporate ownership.

In Iran, political economy is characterized by a "petro-rentier" state model combined with extensive clerical and military patronage. Following the 1979 Revolution, major economic assets were placed under state-affiliated religious foundations (*bonyads*) and later transferred to corporate conglomerates owned by the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), creating a "semi-governmental" (خصولتی) sector. Iran's economy relies heavily on hydrocarbon rents distributed through untargeted consumer subsidies and patronage networks, insulating state rulers from popular tax-based accountability.
