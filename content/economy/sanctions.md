## Definition
Economic sanctions are coercive, non-military foreign policy instruments imposed by one or more sovereign states, or international bodies such as the United Nations, against a target state, entity, or individual. Designed to compel compliance with international norms, alter specific policies, deter aggression, or degrade strategic capabilities, sanctions restrict normal international trade, financial transactions, technological transfers, or diplomatic travel.

Sanctions range from comprehensive embargoes (severing commercial relations) to targeted "smart" sanctions, focusing pressure on officials, military entities, or strategic sectors like energy and finance.

## Why it matters
In contemporary international relations, sanctions have become the primary coercive tool utilized by powerful states below the threshold of direct armed conflict. Governments frequently deploy sanctions to signal moral condemnation, address weapons proliferation, punish treaty breaches, or respond to gross human rights violations without risking military casualties.

However, sanctions raise profound ethical and humanitarian concerns. Broad measures disrupt entire economies, devastating civilians through currency depreciation, job losses, and restricted medical supplies, while entrenching authoritarian cartels through shadow-market smuggling.

## How it works
Modern sanctions operate through sophisticated legal, regulatory, and financial enforcement mechanisms:

1. **Multilateral vs. Unilateral Sanctions**: Multilateral sanctions, authorized by the United Nations Security Council under Chapter VII of the UN Charter, are legally binding on all UN member states. Unilateral sanctions are enacted by individual states (such as the US or EU member states) based on domestic statutory authority.
2. **Financial De-risking and Swift Disconnection**: Modern sanctions heavily target the global banking infrastructure. Banning targeted banks from SWIFT (the global financial messaging system) and cutting them off from the US dollar clearing system paralyzes international trade, as foreign banks terminate all transactions out of fear of regulatory punishment.
3. **Secondary Sanctions**: The extraterritorial enforcement of domestic sanctions legislation, where a sanctioning state (notably the United States) threatens foreign third-party companies with exclusion from its own financial market if they conduct business with the target nation.
4. **Asset Freezes and Travel Bans**: Freezing the foreign-held bank accounts and physical assets of designated individuals and regimes while barring them from entering sanctioning jurisdictions.
5. **Humanitarian Exemptions and Overcompliance**: While most sanction regimes explicitly provide legal exemptions for medicine, food, and medical devices, international banks and shipping companies frequently refuse to process permitted humanitarian goods out of fear of inadvertent violations (overcompliance).

## Contested points
The efficacy and ethical legitimacy of economic sanctions are the subject of persistent international debate.

Proponents argue that sanctions provide an indispensable, proportionate alternative between toothless diplomatic statements and devastating military invasions. They argue that targeted sanctions impose direct financial costs on autocratic elites, deter future aggression by signaling collective resolve, and successfully compel negotiated compromises on critical security matters, such as non-proliferation treaties.

Conversely, humanitarian critics and international legal scholars argue that broad economic sanctions constitute collective punishment, disproportionately harming vulnerable civilians—children, the sick, and the poor—while autocratic elites remain insulated through black-market monopolies. They argue that sanctions rarely achieve their stated objective of regime change or fundamental foreign policy reversal; instead, target regimes routinely externalise economic costs onto their citizens and weaponise foreign pressure to justify domestic political repression.

Another debate surrounds extraterritoriality: many states view unilateral secondary sanctions as an unlawful infringement on sovereign jurisdiction, challenging the hegemony of the US dollar.

## Examples
The international sanctions against apartheid South Africa in the 1970s and 1980s illustrate coercive success: multilateral arms embargoes, boycotts, and divestment campaigns imposed mounting costs on the white-minority government, aiding the democratic transition in 1994.

Conversely, the comprehensive UN sanctions imposed on Iraq following the 1990 Gulf War had catastrophic humanitarian consequences. The near-total trade embargo caused hyperinflation, infrastructure collapse, and widespread malnutrition, prompting intense global ethical condemnation and driving the international shift toward "targeted" sanctions.

In Iran, international and unilateral sanctions have profoundly shaped political economy and daily life for decades. Following the 1979 hostage crisis, the US imposed unilateral sanctions, which expanded in the late 2000s with UN Security Council resolutions over Iran's nuclear program. The 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA) granted temporary sanctions relief, but the unilateral US withdrawal in 2018 reimposed "maximum pressure" secondary sanctions, locking Iran out of global banking and oil markets. While the regime did not collapse, the resulting currency crash, triple-digit inflation for imported goods, and medicine shortages severely degraded middle-class livelihoods while enriching state-aligned shadow-economy cartels.
