## Definition
Capitalism is an economic system based on private ownership of the means of production, voluntary exchange, wage labour, and the coordination of production and consumption through decentralized price mechanisms in competitive markets. In a capitalist economy, investment decisions, resource allocation, and technological innovation are driven primarily by the pursuit of capital accumulation and profit.

Markets are social and institutional arrangements where buyers and sellers interact to exchange goods, services, and assets. While markets have existed across human history in ancient and medieval societies, capitalism emerged when markets expanded to commodify fundamental elements of human existence: land, labour, and money.

## Why it matters
Capitalism is the dominant economic system structuring global society today. Over the past two centuries, capitalist industrialisation has driven unprecedented increases in material wealth, scientific advancement, lifespans, and productive capacity across the world.

However, capitalist development has also generated profound political and social tensions. It produces recurring cycles of financial instability, structural wealth inequality, and environmental degradation. The collision between the capitalist imperative for continuous market expansion and the democratic demand for social security and environmental protection constitutes the primary dynamic of modern politics.

## How it works
Capitalist market economies operate through several interlocking institutional pillars:

1. **Private Property and Contract Law**: Secure, legally enforceable property rights allow individuals and corporations to own capital, enter legally binding agreements, and retain profits.
2. **The Price Mechanism**: Prices determined by supply and demand act as an information system, signaling scarcity and abundance. Rising prices incentivise suppliers to expand production, while falling prices redirect investment.
3. **Wage Labour**: The majority of the population, lacking sufficient capital assets to live independently, sells its labour power to employers in exchange for wages or salaries.
4. **Capital Accumulation and Competition**: Firms compete for consumer market share. To survive competitive pressure, businesses are compelled to reinvest profits into technological innovation, productivity improvements, and cost reduction.
5. **The Financial System**: Banks, stock exchanges, and bond markets mobilize societal savings and allocate investment capital to entrepreneurial ventures and corporations.

## Contested points
The moral legitimacy and long-term viability of capitalism remain subject to fundamental disputes.

Proponents of free-market capitalism, within classical liberal and neoliberal traditions, argue that capitalism is the only economic system compatible with individual liberty and efficient resource distribution. They argue that voluntary market transactions create mutual gains, that the profit motive unleashes human creativity, and that competitive markets democratise consumption by producing higher-quality goods at lower costs.

Critics from the socialist and Marxist traditions contend that capitalism is fundamentally exploitative and contradictory. They argue that profit is extracted from the unpaid labour of workers, that market competition inevitably leads to corporate monopolisation, and that the relentless drive for capital accumulation treats human beings and planetary ecosystems as disposable commodities.

A third major position, articulated in social democratic and institutionalist economics (such as the "varieties of capitalism" literature), argues that capitalism is not monolithic. They distinguish between deregulated "Liberal Market Economies" (such as the US and UK) and "Coordinated Market Economies" (such as Germany and the Nordic states), which integrate robust collective bargaining, codetermination in corporate boards, and comprehensive welfare systems to balance market efficiency with social equity.

## Examples
The Industrial Revolution in 18th- and 19th-century Great Britain marked the historic transition to industrial capitalism. The enclosure of common lands forced rural peasants into growing industrial cities to work in textile factories, generating immense productive output while creating harsh working conditions that prompted the rise of trade unions and factory legislation.

In the late 20th century, the transformation of China under Deng Xiaoping after 1978 illustrated a state-directed variant of capitalism, termed "socialism with Chinese characteristics." By establishing special economic zones, welcoming foreign private investment, and allowing domestic entrepreneurship while maintaining Communist Party political dominance, China lifted hundreds of millions out of extreme poverty and became the world's second-largest economy.

In Iran, modern capitalism has been shaped by oil dependency, state intervention, and post-revolutionary nationalisation. Following the 1979 Revolution, banks and major manufacturing enterprises were nationalised. During the 1990s and 2000s, privatization initiatives under Article 44 of the Constitution did not lead to competitive private markets; instead, state assets were acquired by quasi-governmental bonyads and military-industrial conglomerates, resulting in a crony capitalist environment marked by monopolies, speculative banking, and worker strikes in sectors like oil and petrochemicals.
