## Definition

Privacy is the fundamental human right of individuals to maintain an autonomous private sphere free from arbitrary interference, unconsented intrusion, or public exposure by the [state](../foundations/the-state) or private entities. Codified in Article 12 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR, 1948) and Article 17 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR, 1966), it encompasses bodily integrity, home sanctity, correspondence secrecy, and informational privacy (data protection). Surveillance is the systematic observation, interception, monitoring, and analysis of the movements, communications, associations, and digital data of individuals or entire populations (Westin, 1967; Lyon, 2007).

## Why it matters

Privacy is the indispensable shield that makes individual autonomy, freedom of thought, and democratic dissent possible. When citizens know they are continuously watched, recorded, or tracked by security agencies or corporate algorithms, a "chilling effect" sets in: individuals censor their political speech, avoid dissenting associations, and conform to state expectations. In the 21st-century digital landscape, state mass surveillance and corporate "surveillance capitalism" have concentrated unprecedented power, threatening the foundations of constitutional democracy and the [rule of law](rights-and-rule-of-law).

## How it works

### Core dimensions of the right to privacy

Constitutional jurisprudence and international human rights law divide privacy into four distinct domains (Westin, 1967; Bygrave, 2002):

- **Physical and territorial privacy**: protection against arbitrary bodily searches and warrantless searches of homes and offices (e.g., Fourth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution).
- **Communication privacy**: secrecy of postal mail, telephone calls, electronic messaging, and encrypted communications.
- **Informational privacy (Data protection)**: the right of individuals to control how their personal data (biometrics, medical history, location traces, financial transactions) is collected, stored, processed, and shared.
- **Decisional privacy**: autonomy over intimate personal choices, such as relationships, reproductive decisions, and medical treatments.

### Targeted vs. Mass surveillance

- **Targeted surveillance**: lawful monitoring directed at a specific named individual suspected of a serious crime or espionage, authorized by an independent judge via a specific, time-limited judicial warrant based on probable cause.
- **Mass surveillance (Bulk interception)**: indiscriminate, dragnet collection and algorithmic analysis of the electronic communications and metadata of millions of innocent citizens without individual suspicion or judicial warrants (uncovered globally by whistleblower Edward Snowden in 2013).

## Contested points

- **Security vs. Liberty**: the primary justification offered by intelligence and police agencies for expansive surveillance powers is preventing terrorism, child exploitation, and cyber warfare. Civil liberties advocates counter that indiscriminate mass surveillance fails to stop attacks while systematically dismantling the constitutional presumption of innocence.
- **End-to-End Encryption**: governments frequently demand legal backdoors or client-side scanning to intercept encrypted messaging (e.g., Signal, WhatsApp) for national security investigations. Cryptographers and human rights defenders reply that backdoors mathematically undermine cybersecurity for all users, leaving activists, journalists, and banks vulnerable to hostile state hackers.
- **Surveillance Capitalism**: social theorist Shoshana Zuboff (2019) argues that digital tech monopolies have built a new economic order based on extracting behavioral data from private online lives, using algorithmic prediction models to shape consumer and voter behavior without democratic oversight.

## Examples

- **Stasi in East Germany (1950–1989)**: the Ministry for State Security established history's most pervasive domestic surveillance apparatus, employing over 90,000 officers and 170,000 citizen informants (*Inoffizielle Mitarbeiter*) to maintain secret files on one-third of the East German population.
- **European Union (GDPR, 2018)**: the General Data Protection Regulation established the world's most rigorous legal framework for informational privacy, giving citizens rights to access, correct, and delete personal data ("right to be forgotten") backed by massive regulatory fines.
- **Iran**: digital surveillance and communication interception are institutionalized across state telecommunications. The government has developed a "National Information Network" (*Shabakeh-ye Melli-ye Ettela'at*), allowing central authorities to throttle or disconnect international internet access during protests, track citizen traffic on state-approved domestic messaging platforms, and monitor mobile geolocation through state telecom providers.
