## Definition

Rights are entitlements — legal, moral, or both — that protect individuals and groups from arbitrary [power](../foundations/power-and-authority). They define what people may do, possess, or be shielded from, regardless of who holds public office. The rule of law is the principle that law, rather than individual rulers, governs society: every person and authority is subject to the law; laws are publicly declared, clear, prospective, and enforced equally. Rights provide the moral substance of a political community; the rule of law guarantees its institutional protection.

## Why it matters

Without rights, people are vulnerable to state oppression and unchecked power. Without the rule of law, constitutional guarantees remain empty promises. These principles reinforce one another: rights define the boundaries of legitimate state action, and the rule of law ensures that those boundaries are upheld in practice. International human rights treaties and legal history treat basic human rights and legal accountability as foundational benchmarks for political governance.

## How it works

### Categories of rights

Human rights are commonly classified into complementary domains:

- **Civil and political rights**: protections including the right to life, freedom from torture, free speech, freedom of assembly, fair trial standards, and political participation. These are codified in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR, 1948) and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR, 1966).
- **Economic, social, and cultural rights**: entitlements to healthcare, education, adequate housing, and labour protections, primarily set out in the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR, 1966).
- **Group and collective rights**: rights held collectively, such as self-determination and the protection of cultural and religious minorities.

The 1993 Vienna Declaration reaffirmed that all human rights are "universal, indivisible and interdependent and interrelated" (Part I, para. 5).

### Core elements of the rule of law

The rule of law is not merely "rule by law" — governments cannot satisfy it simply by enacting repressive decrees. It requires several distinct institutional elements:

1. **Supremacy of law**: no individual, including the highest official, is above the law.
2. **Clarity and stability**: rules must be prospective, understandable, and publicly accessible.
3. **Equal application**: the law applies equally to governors and the governed.
4. **Judicial independence**: courts must decide matters free from executive or legislative interference. (See [Separation of powers](../institutions/separation-of-powers).)
5. **Procedural fairness**: due process requires fair, public hearings before impartial tribunals prior to any deprivation of liberty or property.

### Binding international frameworks

- **UDHR (1948)**: adopted by the UN General Assembly; serves as the foundation of modern human rights standards.
- **ICCPR (1966)** & **ICESCR (1966)**: legally binding multilateral covenants, ratified by over 170 states, including Iran (ratified in 1975).

## Contested points

- **Universality vs. cultural relativism**: universalists maintain that fundamental rights apply to every human being across all cultures. Proponents of cultural relativism contend that rights must reflect historical, cultural, and religious traditions, a position voiced at the 1993 Bangkok regional meeting. The Vienna Declaration confirmed universality while acknowledging regional contexts.
- **Negative vs. positive rights**: negative rights demand state non-interference (e.g., freedom from censorship), while positive rights obligate the state to provide resources (e.g., public healthcare). While some critics argue positive rights are aspirational policy goals, international covenants treat both as binding legal standards.
- **The implementation gap**: widespread tension persists between formal legal ratification and domestic enforcement. International mechanisms rely heavily on national judiciaries and civil society to hold governments accountable.

## Examples

- **Magna Carta, 1215**: established early limits on monarchical authority, affirming that free men could not be imprisoned except by lawful judgment of peers.
- **South Africa, 1996**: the post-apartheid [constitution](../institutions/constitutions) entrenched both civil liberties and justiciable socioeconomic rights; in *Government of the Republic of South Africa v. Grootboom* (2000), the Constitutional Court ordered the state to address emergency housing needs.
- **Iran**: Articles 19 through 42 of the constitution outline rights including equality before the law and freedom from arbitrary detention, while establishing that all legislation must adhere to Islamic standards vetted by the Guardian Council. The reconciliation between domestic religious criteria and international covenant commitments remains an ongoing subject of domestic and international debate.
