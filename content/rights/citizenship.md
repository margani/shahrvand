## Definition

Citizenship is the formal legal and political status conferring full membership in a sovereign [state](../foundations/the-state). It establishes a reciprocal relationship between the individual and the state: the state guarantees the citizen civil, political, and social rights alongside international diplomatic protection, while the citizen owes allegiance, compliance with statutory laws, and civic duties (such as taxation, jury service, or military conscription). In political theory, citizenship encompasses both a formal legal title (*status*) and an active ethical practice of participation in democratic self-governance (Marshall, 1950; Shachar, 2009).

## Why it matters

Citizenship functions as what political philosopher Hannah Arendt termed "the right to have rights" (Arendt, 1951). Without legal citizenship, individuals are rendered stateless — stripped of passports, denied legal employment, excluded from public healthcare and education, and vulnerable to arbitrary deportation. Furthermore, citizenship defines the boundaries of the democratic community: it determines who has the constitutional right to vote in [elections](../institutions/elections-and-voting-systems), stand for public office, and shape the laws of the land.

## How it works

### T. H. Marshall's three dimensions of citizenship

In his landmark study *Citizenship and Social Class* (1950), British sociologist T. H. Marshall traced the historical development of citizenship rights across three centuries:

- **Civil citizenship (18th century)**: rights necessary for individual freedom — liberty of person, freedom of speech, thought and faith, the right to own property and conclude valid contracts, and the right to justice through courts.
- **Political citizenship (19th century)**: the right to participate in the exercise of political power — universal suffrage and the right to stand for elected office.
- **Social citizenship (20th century)**: the right to a minimum standard of economic welfare and security, realized through public education, universal healthcare, and unemployment insurance within the [welfare state](../foundations/socialism-and-social-democracy).

### Modes of acquiring citizenship

Under comparative nationality law, states grant citizenship at birth through two distinct doctrines:
- **Jus soli (Right of the soil)**: citizenship is automatically granted to any child born within the territorial borders of the state, regardless of the parents' nationality or legal status (common in the Americas, including the United States under the Fourteenth Amendment).
- **Jus sanguinis (Right of blood)**: citizenship is inherited through the nationality of the parents, regardless of where the child is born (common in Europe, Asia, and the Middle East).

Non-citizens can also acquire citizenship through **naturalization**, a legal procedure requiring lawful residency, language proficiency, civic tests, and background checks.

## Contested points

- **Liberal vs. Republican conceptions**: liberal political theory views citizenship primarily as a passive package of negative legal entitlements protecting private individuals from state interference. Civic republicanism (Pocock, 1998) views citizenship as an active moral duty of public participation, arguing that democracy decays when citizens retreat into private consumerism and abandon civic duties.
- **Birthright lottery vs. Global justice**: legal theorist Ayelet Shachar (2009) argues that allocating citizenship by the accidental circumstances of birth functions like feudal inherited property, perpetuating vast global inequalities. Cosmopolitans advocate for more porous borders and easier pathways to legal status for economic migrants and refugees.
- **Dual citizenship and loyalty**: while most democracies now permit dual nationality, traditional nationalists argue that holding multiple passports dilutes civic loyalty, creating conflicts of allegiance during international crises.

## Examples

- **Ancient Athens vs. Rome**: Athens practiced an exclusionary, highly participatory direct citizenship restricted to free native males; the Roman Republic and Empire transformed citizenship into a legal status (*civitas*) that could be extended to conquered peoples, providing legal protection across imperial territories.
- **Statelessness and the Rohingya**: stripped of Myanmar citizenship under the 1982 Citizenship Law, over one million Rohingya people were rendered stateless, denying them travel documents, higher education, and legal protection, culminating in mass displacement in 2017.
- **Iran**: citizenship is governed under the Civil Code primarily by *jus sanguinis* (Article 976), granting Iranian nationality automatically to children of Iranian fathers. Until recent legal reforms in 2019, Iranian mothers married to foreign nationals (such as Afghan refugees) could not pass citizenship to their children born in Iran, creating widespread documentation and residency vulnerabilities for thousands of children.
