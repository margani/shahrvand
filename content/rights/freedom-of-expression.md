## Definition

Freedom of expression is the fundamental human right of every individual to hold opinions without interference and to seek, receive, and impart information and ideas of all kinds, regardless of frontiers, through any media of their choice. Codified in Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR, 1948) and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR, 1966), it encompasses spoken and written words, artistic creation, peaceful assembly, and digital communication on the internet (Mill, 1859; Barendt, 2005).

## Why it matters

Freedom of expression is described by constitutional courts as the essential foundation of a democratic society. Without it, citizens cannot expose official corruption, debate public policies, or cast informed votes during [elections](../institutions/elections-and-voting-systems). It serves as a necessary prerequisite for the defense of all other human rights: when speech is silenced, arbitrary detentions and abuses of power cannot be publicly scrutinized or corrected.

## How it works

### Philosophical justifications

Philosophers offer three foundational arguments defending free expression (Mill, 1859; Meiklejohn, 1948):

- **The search for truth (The marketplace of ideas)**: in *On Liberty* (1859), John Stuart Mill argued that silencing any opinion deprives society of truth. An opinion may be true; if partially true, debate refines it; even if false, confronting it prevents true beliefs from becoming unthinking dogma.
- **Democratic self-governance**: Alexander Meiklejohn argued that democracy requires an informed electorate. For citizens to rule themselves legitimately, all public issues must be openly discussed without government viewpoint discrimination.
- **Individual autonomy**: the freedom to express one's thoughts and artistic creativity is an intrinsic requirement of human dignity, independent of its political utility.

### Lawful restrictions: the three-part test

Freedom of expression is not absolute. Under international law (ICCPR, Article 19(3)), states may restrict expression only if the measure satisfies a strict three-part cumulative test:
1. **Legality**: the restriction must be explicitly provided by clear, accessible statutory law.
2. **Legitimate aim**: it must protect one of five enumerated interests: the rights of others, national security, public order, public health, or public morals.
3. **Necessity and proportionality**: it must be strictly necessary in a democratic society, using the least restrictive measure possible. Article 20 additionally mandates prohibitions on advocacy of national, racial, or religious hatred that constitutes incitement to discrimination, hostility, or violence.

## Contested points

- **Hate speech vs. Free speech**: democracies draw differing lines. In the United States, under *Brandenburg v. Ohio* (1969), inflammatory hate speech is protected unless it incites "imminent lawless action." In Europe, Canada, and South Africa, laws criminalize hate speech, Holocaust denial, and group defamation, prioritizing human dignity and minority protection over absolute speech rights.
- **Disinformation and digital platforms**: the proliferation of digital misinformation and state propaganda has sparked intense debate. Critics argue online falsehoods erode democracy, while civil libertarians warn that granting governments power to define "fake news" inevitably leads to censorship of opposition.
- **National security and whistleblowing**: the tension between state secrecy and publishing evidence of government illegality remains acute. Governments prosecute leakers under espionage statutes, while media advocates defend whistleblowing as an act of public accountability.

## Examples

- **United States (*New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 1964)**: the Supreme Court established that public officials cannot win libel damages for critical reporting unless they prove "actual malice," safeguarding aggressive investigative journalism.
- **European Court of Human Rights (*Handyside v. United Kingdom*, 1976)**: established that freedom of expression applies not only to favorable ideas, but also to those that "offend, shock, or disturb the State or any sector of the population."
- **Iran**: Article 24 of the Constitution guarantees press freedom "except when detrimental to the fundamental principles of Islam or public rights." In practice, the Press Supervisory Board, specialized Media Courts, and internet filtering restrict critical publications, block major digital platforms, and prosecute journalists under state security laws.
