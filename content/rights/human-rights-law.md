## Definition

International human rights law is the body of international treaties, customary international legal norms, declarations, and institutional mechanisms that establish legally binding obligations on sovereign [states](../foundations/the-state) to respect, protect, and fulfill the fundamental rights and freedoms of all human beings within their jurisdiction. Catalyzed by the atrocities of World War II and the Holocaust, it established for the first time in human history that a state's treatment of its own inhabitants is a legitimate matter of international legal concern, superseding traditional doctrines of unfettered state sovereignty (Henkin, 1990; Donnelly, 2013).

## Why it matters

Before the development of modern international human rights law, sovereign states claimed absolute domestic jurisdiction over their subjects; how a ruler treated their citizens was legally their exclusive internal affair. The international human rights framework created an objective global moral and legal benchmark. When domestic courts or national [constitutions](../institutions/constitutions) fail to protect citizens against tyranny, international covenants provide legal standards, universal monitoring committees, and regional courts where victims can document abuses and hold power-holders accountable.

## How it works

### The International Bill of Human Rights

The constitutional foundation of international human rights law rests on three core instruments, collectively known as the International Bill of Human Rights:

- **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR, 1948)**: adopted by the UN General Assembly without dissent; a comprehensive moral manifesto whose 30 articles define the universality of civil, political, economic, social, and cultural rights. While adopted as a non-binding resolution, large portions are today recognized as binding customary international law.
- **International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR, 1966)**: a legally binding multilateral treaty protecting life, prohibiting torture and slavery, guaranteeing fair trials, and protecting freedoms of thought, assembly, and speech (ratified by 173 states as of November 2026).
- **International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR, 1966)**: a legally binding treaty guaranteeing rights to fair labor conditions, education, healthcare, social security, and adequate housing (ratified by 171 states).

### Monitoring and Enforcement Mechanisms

International human rights are monitored through several parallel channels:
- **UN Treaty Bodies**: independent expert committees (e.g., the Human Rights Committee) that review periodic state compliance reports, publish General Comments interpreting covenant articles, and examine individual complaints against ratifying states.
- **Universal Periodic Review (UPR)**: a peer-review mechanism under the UN Human Rights Council where every UN member state's human rights record is scrutinized every four and a half years.
- **Regional Human Rights Courts**: courts with legally binding judicial powers over member states, notably the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) and the Inter-American Court of Human Rights (IACtHR).

## Contested points

- **Universality vs. Cultural Relativism**: universalists assert that fundamental human rights belong to all individuals by virtue of their shared humanity. Proponents of cultural relativism (such as governments supporting the 1993 Bangkok Declaration) argue that international norms reflect Western secular individualism, demanding that human rights be interpreted in light of national, religious, and cultural contexts. The 1993 Vienna Declaration affirmed that all human rights are universal and indivisible, while acknowledging regional particularities.
- **Enforcement weakness**: critics argue that international human rights law lacks an effective global police force, allowing powerful states to ignore international condemnation with impunity. Defenders respond that international law works through "naming and shaming," domestic judicial incorporation, trade conditions, and empowering civil society watchdogs.
- **First-generation vs. Second-generation rights**: debates continue over whether civil-political rights (which require state restraint) and economic-social rights (which require progressive state resource investment) possess the same legal status and judicial enforceability.

## Examples

- **Nuremberg Trials (1945–1946)**: established the revolutionary international legal precedent that state officials, including heads of state, bear individual criminal responsibility under international law for crimes against humanity, rejecting the defense of "following superior orders."
- **European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR)**: the world's most effective international human rights court; private citizens in 46 member states can sue their own governments in Strasbourg, resulting in binding judgments reforming domestic surveillance laws, criminal justice procedures, and detention standards.
- **Iran**: Iran is a founding signatory of the UDHR (1948) and formally ratified both the ICCPR and ICESCR in 1975 under the Pahlavi government, remaining a state party after the 1979 Revolution. However, tensions regularly arise between Iran's international treaty commitments and domestic legal codes (such as the Islamic Penal Code and laws on religious apostasy, women's legal status, and freedom of assembly), which Iranian authorities justify by asserting the constitutional supremacy of Islamic criteria.
